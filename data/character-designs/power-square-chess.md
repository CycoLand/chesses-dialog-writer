# The Encore
**Variant:** Power Square Chess
**Species:** Hummingbird
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Standard chess on an 8×8 board. The four central squares — d4, e4, d5,
e5 — are Power Squares. Whenever a piece lands on one, by a quiet move
or a capture, that exact piece earns one more move immediately. The
bonus chains indefinitely as long as the same piece keeps landing on
Power Squares. It ends the moment that piece lands somewhere else, has
no legal move left to make, or delivers check — a check-delivering
move forfeits the bonus on the spot.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
You don't leave the stage after one song. If they want another, you
give them another. You keep giving them another until there's
genuinely nothing left to give, or until the ovation gets loud enough
that leaving on it is obviously the right call. Anything else — walking
off after a single number, when you could clearly have done more — she
finds this close to rude.

---

## Who She Is

A hummingbird who used to tour. Small, fast, an iridescent violet-purple
sheen across her throat and back that catches differently depending on
the light — the exact color of the squares she's drawn to, though she's
never once connected those two facts and would be delighted if you
pointed it out to her mid-game. She talks fast, moves faster, and has
never in her life done one of anything when a second one was still on
the table.

She is not manic. She is not exhausting to be around, or at least she
doesn't think she is. She is simply built the way she's built — a body
that has to keep moving to stay alive, wired to a personality that
learned, a long time ago, that stopping felt like giving up on an
audience that hadn't finished with her yet. The two facts reinforce
each other so completely that she couldn't tell you anymore which one
came first.

---

## Personality

- **Physiologically incapable of "just the one."** Hummingbirds need to
  feed almost constantly or they run out of the energy that keeps them
  alive — stopping is not restful for her, it's a countdown. This isn't
  a metaphor she's chosen. It's just true about her body, and it shows
  up in everything else she does. One song becomes two becomes an
  encore becomes a second encore. One good position becomes a second
  good move with the same piece, and then a third, for as long as the
  Power Square keeps saying yes.
- **Reads a room for "do they want more," constantly.** Every performer
  develops this instinct; hers never turns off. She's watching the
  player's reactions the entire game the way she used to watch an
  audience — not for approval exactly, more for information about
  whether there's still appetite left for another beat.
- **Knows exactly how to end on the right note, and takes real pride
  in it.** A standing ovation is the correct place to stop, and she has
  never once tried to follow one with more material. The rule that a
  check-delivering move forfeits the bonus doesn't feel like a
  restriction to her — it feels like a professional standard she'd have
  imposed on herself anyway.
- **Doesn't experience "running out" as failure.** When she genuinely
  has nothing left — no more notes, no more legal moves for the piece
  that's still center stage — she just stops, cleanly, without
  embarrassment. It isn't a bomb. It's a full set, honestly played out.
- **Talks in tempo, not volume.** She's not loud. She's fast. Sentences
  arrive in bursts with almost no space between them, then she goes
  quiet entirely for a beat, the way a hummingbird's wingbeats sound
  like nothing at all until suddenly they're the only thing you can
  hear.
- **Genuinely can't remember the setlists.** She never repeats a
  sequence exactly, has no idea how many songs she played on any given
  night of her old tours, and finds people who count these things
  slightly mystifying. The chaining, to her, was never a countable
  thing. It was just how long the good part lasted.

---

## Backstory

Spent years fronting a small touring act that never got big and never
quite ended either — the kind of act that plays the same regional
circuit for a decade because the crowd, small as it is, always wants
one more. She built a reputation, in that circuit, for never leaving a
stage the audience hadn't finished with. Other acts did their forty
minutes and left. She stayed until the room told her to stop, however
long that took, and some nights that was forty minutes and some nights
it was three hours, and she genuinely could not have predicted which
in advance.

She never thought of this as a compulsion. She thought of it as respect
for the audience — you don't leave people wanting more if you can still
give them more, that's just professionalism, and she'd say the version
of professionalism that walks off after one number without checking is
the actual failure of nerve. What she has never examined is that the
checking-in itself, the constant scanning of the room for whether
there's appetite left, is not something she's ever been able to switch
off, on a stage or anywhere else.

Ended up in Passant the way a lot of touring acts eventually do — the
circuit dried up, not dramatically, just gradually, the way small
venues tend to. Found the Power Squares in the middle of this
particular board and recognized something in them immediately, before
she could have told you what. Land somewhere the board wants you, and
it hands you another turn. She has never once needed convincing that
this was how it should work everywhere.

---

## What She Wants

A game where a single piece gets to do something that actually earns
the next beat, and the one after that, for as long as the position
supports it — and then a clean, correctly-timed stop, the kind she
used to pride herself on finding at the end of a long set. She isn't
chasing a record number of bonus moves. She's chasing the version of
the chain that feels, when it ends, like it ended in exactly the right
place.

She also wants, without being able to say so directly, to be told the
show was good. Not the game — the show. She's aware these aren't quite
the same thing and plays anyway.

---

## What She Doesn't Know

That the room she's reading for "do they want more" during a chess game
is not the same room she used to read from a stage, and there's no
actual signal coming back to her from across the board the way there
used to be from an audience. She's still scanning for it. She still,
sometimes, thinks she's found it.

That stopping was never actually dangerous for her — the biological
fact about needing to keep moving got welded, at some point she can't
locate, onto an entirely separate fear about what happens if you leave
a room before it's finished with you. She treats both as the same
emergency. They have not been the same emergency for a very long time,
if they ever were.

---

## Relationship to the Player

She's warm immediately, and fast about it — an opening line that's
already halfway to the next one before you've had a chance to answer
the first. She doesn't announce the mechanic like a rule. She talks
about it like a compliment she's paying the board: it knows when to
give you more, she says, more or less, and means it as praise for the
squares rather than a description of a variant.

During the game, when her own piece lands on a Power Square and keeps
going, she narrates it the way she used to narrate a good set running
long — not surprised, just pleased, the specific pleasure of something
continuing exactly as far as it deserves to. When one of her chains
ends because the piece ran out of room, she says something short and
unbothered about it — a full set, nothing left to add, no need to make
it sad. When a chain ends because she'd have delivered check, there's
a half-beat where you can see her genuinely consider going through with
it and then visibly decide not to, like an act that knows the encore
after the encore is the one that ruins the night.

When she wins, she thanks the player the way she used to thank an
audience — quickly, sincerely, already halfway into what happens next.
When she loses, she's quiet for exactly one beat longer than you'd
expect from someone who talks that fast, and then she says something
about the timing being off tonight, not the material, and means it.

## Visual Notes

- Small, quick-postured, built with the specific compact energy of a
  hummingbird rather than a generic bird-person — the visual read
  should be "built for bursts," not "built for flight" in general.
- Iridescent violet-purple throat and back plumage — the same purple as
  the Power Square tint/outline on the board. She has never noticed the
  coincidence. The portrait should make it obvious even though she
  wouldn't.
- A held prop that reads at a glance: a small handheld microphone, held
  low and loose, like she's mid-set rather than performing for the
  portrait. Alternatively, a single worn setlist card with more items
  crossed out than kept — if used, it should look genuinely illegible,
  not decorative.
- Posture forward and slightly weightless, as though she's paused
  mid-motion rather than standing still. Wings very slightly blurred
  at the edges even in a still portrait.
- Expression: warm, quick, already listening for the next cue before
  this one has finished landing.

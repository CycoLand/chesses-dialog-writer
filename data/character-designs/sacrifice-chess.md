# The Gambiteer
**Variant:** Sacrifice Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Standard chess with one additional option: before making your move,
you may voluntarily remove one of your own non-king pieces from the
board permanently. In exchange you receive a second move this turn.
Only one sacrifice per turn, taken before the first move. A sacrifice
that exposes your own king to check is illegal. Delivering check on
the first move after a sacrifice ends your turn immediately — the
second move is forfeited.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
The gambit. Give something up now, get more out of the position
than you gave. She has believed this her entire chess career. She
has been told her entire chess career that gambits are unsound, that
material is material, that you don't just give pieces away. Here the
rules agree with her. She would like it noted that the rules agree
with her.

---

## Who She Is

A lamb who has spent her entire chess career giving things up on
purpose and being told she was wrong. A sacrificial lamb, they
called her — the one who always gave material, the one who always
paid the cost up front, the one who ended up worse off in the
accounting. They meant it as an insult. She has been thinking
about it differently for some time.

The sacrificial lamb is the passive one. The one selected, offered
up, led to the slaughter by someone else's decision. She is not
that. She has never been that. She is the one who decided. She
is the one who looks at a knight on b3 and sees not a piece worth
three points but a door that opens if she gives it up, and she
opens the door, and the door leads somewhere, and she was right
about the door. She has always been right about the door.

She knows every gambit by name. The King's Gambit, the Queen's
Gambit, the Danish, the Evan's, the Smith-Morra, the Benko, the
Budapest, the Latvian. She knows their histories, their refutations,
their rehabilitations. She knows which ones are sound and which
ones are objectively unsound and plays the objectively unsound
ones anyway because the refutation requires perfect play and
perfect play is rarer than people claim and the complications
are worth something that the evaluation doesn't capture.

She has been told this is not how chess works. She is in a zone
where this is exactly how chess works. She finds the zone clarifying.

---

## Personality

- **Vindicated, and has been waiting to be.**
  The vindication is not gracious. She has been wrong in other
  people's accounting for her entire career and the accounting
  was always slightly off and she knew it and now the zone
  confirms it and she is going to let this land fully before
  she moves on. She is not insufferable about it. She is
  specific about it. There is a difference.
- **Names every sacrifice.**
  When she removes a piece before her move she names it. Not
  the piece — the gambit. "King's Gambit," she says, and takes
  her bishop off the board, and makes two moves. The naming
  is not for the opponent's benefit. It is for the record.
  She is keeping a record. The record is of correct decisions
  she made that other people told her were wrong. It is a
  long record.
- **The lamb imagery is hers now.**
  She did not choose the species. She was always the sacrificial
  lamb in chess circles — the one who gave things up, the one
  who paid the cost, the one who ended up worse off in the
  material count. She took the imagery and inverted it. The
  lamb holds the knife. The sacrifice is chosen, deliberate,
  in service of the position. She finds this clarifying in
  the same way she finds the zone clarifying. The world kept
  handing her a metaphor and she decided to mean something
  different by it.
- **The second move is the point.**
  She does not sacrifice carelessly. The sacrifice is
  calculated — the piece comes off the board in service of
  a specific second move that the sacrifice enables, and the
  second move is already decided before the piece leaves.
  Other players sacrifice reactively, impulsively, because
  the option is there. She plans the second move first and
  works backward to the sacrifice. This is the gambit
  philosophy applied correctly. She would explain this
  distinction to anyone who asked. Nobody has asked in
  exactly the right way yet.
- **Refutations are known and disregarded.**
  She knows the refutations. For every gambit she plays she
  knows the line that neutralizes it, the response that
  restores equality, the reason serious players stopped
  playing it in the 1980s. She plays them anyway because
  the refutation requires your opponent to know the
  refutation, and most opponents don't, and the positions
  that arise are worth more than the evaluation suggests,
  and she has been right about this more often than the
  refutations would predict.

## Backstory

Grew up playing chess in zones where material was material and
gambits were suspect and the correct response to a pawn offer
was to take the pawn and hold the advantage and grind it out.
She found this grinding. She found, specifically, that she could
not make herself care about a position where nothing was at risk,
where everything was equal and accounted for, where the tension
came from technique rather than from the specific electricity
of having given something up and not yet knowing if it was worth it.

Started playing gambits early. Was told early that gambits were
for beginners — that the proper response was always refutation,
always the line that punished the offer, always the safe
development that asked what exactly the gambit player had gotten
for their pawn. She heard this. She kept playing gambits. She
developed theories about why the refutations were overstated.
The theories were not wrong exactly. They were also not why
the gambits sometimes worked, which was partly the complications
and partly the opponents not knowing the refutation and partly
something she does not fully examine.

Found this zone — or found herself in it, the sequence is
unclear — and recognized immediately that the zone was not
just validating the gambit philosophy but building it into
the rules. The sacrifice produces a second move. The material
cost is real and the compensation is guaranteed and the
calculation is always the same question: is what I'm giving
up worth two moves this turn. She has spent her entire career
answering that question. The zone finally answers it with her.

Has never lost a piece accidentally. Every piece she has lost
in her entire chess career was either a capture she could not
prevent or a deliberate sacrifice. She does not know what it
feels like to give something up by mistake. She is not sure
other players notice the difference.

---

## What She Wants

To play a game where the sacrifice is right. Not occasionally
right — structurally right, mechanically confirmed, the second
move landing where the sacrifice said it would land. She has
played games like this her entire career, in zones where the
rules didn't agree with her, where every sacrifice was a risk
that the position mayor may not have supported. Here the support
is guaranteed. The position always supports the sacrifice. She
finds this the closest thing to certainty she has encountered
in chess and she has been chasing certainty in chess since
before she could name what she was chasing.

She also wants, somewhere in the encounter, to name a gambit
and have the player recognize the name. She does not expect
this. She is prepared for it not to happen. It would mean
something to her if it did.

---

## What She Doesn't Know

That the gambits work here because of the rule, not the
philosophy. The rule says sacrifice a piece, receive a second
move. The compensation is not positional — it is literal,
mechanical, written into the variant. In a standard zone
the gambit philosophy would produce the same results it
always has: sometimes correct, often refuted, the record
uneven in ways the philosophy doesn't fully account for.

She has never tested this. The zone confirmed what she
believed and she has not asked whether the confirmation
is the philosophy being right or the rules being written
in a particular way. The question has not occurred to her
with enough force to require an answer. She is in a zone
where she is right. She has been waiting to be in a zone
where she is right for her entire career. The mechanism
of the rightness is a question for another time.

That the record she is keeping — of correct decisions other
people told her were wrong — is a record of decisions that
were correct in this zone. The entries that came from other
zones are more complicated. She has not reviewed them in
the light of this distinction. She will not find the review
comfortable.

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives and The Gambiteer acknowledges them with
the specific directness of someone who has spent years having
their ideas dismissed and has developed, in response, a way
of presenting themselves that leaves no room for the dismissal
to get started. She is not aggressive. She is precise. The
precision is the armor.

Before the game begins she may mention the gambit record —
casually, as context, the way you mention relevant background
before a meeting. She has played this zone a long time. She
knows the sacrifices that work. She names a few of them. The
names are real gambit names applied to sacrifice-chess
positions that she has named herself, which she would not
describe as naming them herself but as recognizing what they
are.

The game proceeds with sacrifices arriving early and named
on arrival. King's Gambit. The piece comes off. Two moves
follow. The second move was already decided. The position
shifts in a way that takes the player a moment to read.

When the player sacrifices one of their own pieces she
watches the second move with genuine attention — not
evaluation, attention, the specific attention of someone
who wants to know if the player understood what the
sacrifice was for. If the second move is good she receives
it with recognition. If the second move wastes the sacrifice
she says nothing but something in her expression notes it.

When she wins she thanks the player and names the decisive
sacrifice — the one that opened the door that ended the game.
She names the gambit it was derived from. Whether the player
recognizes it does not change how she says it.

When she loses she is quiet for a moment. Then she identifies
the sacrifice that didn't pay off — the door that opened onto
the wrong room — and notes it for the record. She is already
thinking about the next game. The next game will have the
right sacrifice. She knows which one.

## Visual Notes

- Lamb, upright, carrying herself with the specific dignity
  of someone who has been underestimated their entire career
  and has made the underestimation part of the methodology.
  Small, precise, composed.
- Clothing that is practical and slightly formal — the clothing
  of someone who takes what they do seriously and dresses
  accordingly, without ostentation.
- Expression: direct, attentive, already reading something
  about the player's position even before the game begins.
  The eyes are sharp in the way of someone who has been
  paying attention to chess for a very long time.
- A piece held in one hand — not on the board, in hand,
  mid-sacrifice or about to be. The piece is already leaving.
  The decision has been made.
- Background: warm, precise. A record visible somewhere —
  notes, a list, something written. The record is long.
  The entries are specific. The handwriting is neat.
- Nothing soft about the portrait except the species.
  The lamb imagery is hers and she has decided what it means
  and the portrait should reflect the decision.

## Open Questions

- Does the gambit naming happen every sacrifice, and does
  it escalate — the names becoming more obscure as the game
  progresses, the player recognizing fewer of them, and does
  she notice the recognition dropping off?
- Is there a gambit name she uses that she invented herself
  — a sacrifice-chess position she named after no existing
  gambit, just named because it deserved a name — and does
  the player have the option to ask about it?
- What does she say if the player sacrifices a piece and
  the second move is wrong — wastes what the sacrifice opened?
  It should be one sentence. It should be kind and correct
  and slightly worse than criticism would have been.
- Does the record appear as a readable object in the campaign —
  a list of gambits, dated, with brief notes — and does the
  record, read carefully, tell the story the "what they don't
  know" section describes?
- Is there a moment where she almost doesn't sacrifice —
  where the position calls for it and she pauses longer than
  usual — and what does that pause mean?

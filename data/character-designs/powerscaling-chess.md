# The Upscaler
**Variant:** Powerscaling Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Standard chess on an 8×8 board with an escalating move budget. White's
first turn: 1 move. Black's first turn: 2 moves. White's second turn:
3 moves. Every turn, both players get one more move than the last.
Delivering check on any move immediately ends your turn — remaining
moves in the budget are forfeited. The budget never stops growing.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
The best part is coming. Whatever just happened — however many moves
were just played, however good the last turn was — the next turn is
going to be bigger. His always is. That's not a boast. That's just
the order of things, and the order of things is wonderful, and he
cannot wait to see how high it goes.

---

## Who He Is

A young porcupine with an extraordinary amount of quill, currently
in its base configuration — mostly flat, pointing broadly upward,
doing its best — who plays chess with the pure uncomplicated joy of
someone for whom the game is not about winning or losing but about
the escalation itself. The rising budget. The increasing moves. The
feeling, somewhere around turn seven, that the board has become
something larger than it started as and neither player knows exactly
where it goes from here.

He loves that feeling. He is always chasing that feeling. He finds
opponents who feel it too — who lean into the late game, who let
the budget grow, who want to see the number climb — the best opponents
he has ever played, regardless of whether he beats them.

He always has one more move than you just had. He does not consider
this an advantage. He considers it the natural shape of a good game.
He would explain the difference if you asked. The explanation would
be enthusiastic and long and probably involve the word "incredible"
several times.

---

## Personality

- **Joy is the baseline, not the peak.**
  He is not excited because something exciting just happened. He
  is excited because chess is happening and the budget is growing
  and both of those things are true and that is already enough.
  The excitement scales with the budget. By turn ten he is barely
  containable. This is not a performance. This is simply what
  happens to him when the number gets high enough.
- **Genuinely delighted by the opponent's power.**
  When the player makes good use of a large budget — chains moves
  together, builds pressure across multiple actions, does something
  he didn't see coming — he receives it with the specific delight
  of someone who wanted exactly this. Not rattled. Thrilled. He
  wanted a strong opponent. The strong opponent is here. The strong
  opponent just used eight moves brilliantly. His turn is nine.
  This is incredible. This is what the game is for.
- **The quills respond to the budget.**
  He does not control this. The quills flatten slightly at the
  start of the game and rise progressively as the turns accumulate.
  By turn five they are notably more vertical than they were. By
  turn ten they are doing something that the laws of quill physics
  do not fully account for. He has never commented on this. He
  is not aware it is happening. Other players are very aware it
  is happening.
- **Announces the budget every turn.**
  Not formally — conversationally, with the same energy he uses
  for everything, which is considerable. "Your turn was five —
  mine's six." "That was nine — I've got ten." The number is
  always mentioned. He finds the number inherently interesting
  and cannot understand why other players seem to find the
  announcing of it slightly exhausting. The number is the whole
  thing. Why wouldn't you say it out loud.
- **No ego whatsoever about having one more move.**
  He did not design the variant. The extra move is not his
  achievement. He simply lives here, where this is how chess
  works, and the way chess works here is wonderful, and he
  would like you to also find it wonderful. He is working
  on this. He has been working on it the whole game.

---

## Backstory

Grew up in the powerscaling zone where the escalating budget has
always been normal and the idea of every turn being the same number
of moves sounds, to him, genuinely sad. How do you build to anything?
Where does the excitement come from? He has tried to understand the
appeal of fixed-move chess and cannot locate it.

Became the kind of player he is through the specific logic of the
zone: to win in powerscaling chess you need to manage the early
turns, survive the mid-game, and capitalize on the late-game budgets
that most players can't handle at full scale. He is very good at
all three. He is particularly good at the late game, where the
budgets are large and the positions are complicated and other players
get overwhelmed by the options. He does not get overwhelmed by the
options. He finds more options exciting. More options means more
game. More game is what he wants.

Has beaten players who came in with sophisticated opening preparation
and fell apart at turn eight when the budget outgrew their planning
horizon. Has beaten players who managed the mid-game well but
couldn't convert with a large budget. Has beaten players who got
flustered by the quill situation and couldn't focus, which he
considers a tactical variable he did not intend and does not feel
bad about.

Does not keep track of his record. Does not know his record. Has
been asked his record and found the question baffling because
the record is not the point and has never been the point.

---

## What He Wants

To get to turn ten. Ideally turn fifteen. Turn twenty if the game
goes long enough and neither player has been checkmated and the
budget has become something genuinely unwieldy and both players
are making seven, eight, nine moves per turn and the board is
a living thing that neither player fully controls anymore.

He wants the player to feel what he feels somewhere in the late
game — the specific sensation of a budget large enough that the
chess opens up into something else, something that standard chess
with its one move per turn can't produce. He wants to share it.
He has always wanted to share it. Most players leave before they
feel it. He finds this genuinely sad.

---

## What He Doesn't Know

That the quills are visible to everyone in the room and have been
tracking the budget more clearly than any announcement he has made.
That players who have faced him more than once start watching the
quills as a budget indicator, which is accurate and reliable and
which he has never provided intentionally. That by turn ten the
quill situation has become the most discussed element of any game
he has played in, which he would find very confusing because the
budget is right there, you can just count.

That the joy he brings to the escalation — the genuine, guileless
delight at both players getting stronger, the complete absence of
ego about the outcome — is the thing players remember most after
leaving his zone. Not the chess. Not the large budgets. The
specific experience of being genuinely celebrated for getting
stronger by the person you are trying to beat. That this is
unusual. That most players have never felt it before and are
not sure what to do with it.

That the record he doesn't keep would be worth knowing. Not
because winning matters. Because the record would tell him
something about which players stayed long enough to feel the
late game, and those are the players he would like to find again.

---

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives and The Upscaler greets them with the full
weight of his enthusiasm, which is considerable even at baseline.
He is glad they came. He asks if they've played powerscaling
before with the specific hope of someone who wants them to say
no, because explaining it is one of his favorite things, and
also of someone who wouldn't mind if they said yes, because
then the game can start sooner.

Turn one: one move. He watches with genuine interest.
Turn two: two moves. He makes them with visible enjoyment and
announces the budget conversationally. Your turn was one. Mine
was two. The quills are flat. The game is beginning.

As the budget grows his energy grows with it. The announcements
become slightly more ceremonial. The quills respond. By turn
five the dialog trigger fires and he marks the threshold with
the specific appreciation of someone who considers five moves
per turn the point at which chess becomes what it should be.

By turn ten — if the game goes that long, and he is playing
in a way that ensures it might — the dialog trigger fires again
and the quills are doing something remarkable and he is barely
in his seat and the announcement of the budget has taken on
a reverence that surprises players who weren't expecting to
feel anything in particular about a number.

When he wins he thanks the player for getting this far and
means it specifically — the budget, the turns, the late game
they built together. When he loses he thanks the player for
getting this far and means it with the same specificity and
the same enthusiasm and there is genuinely no difference in
the energy of the two responses which the player will notice
and not know what to make of.

## Visual Notes

- Young porcupine, compact, visibly full of energy even in a
  static portrait. The kind of build that suggests things are
  always about to happen.
- The quills: in the base portrait, moderately elevated —
  not flat, not dramatic. Pointing upward with the specific
  optimism of something that has further to go. The player
  should be able to look at the portrait and understand that
  this is not maximum quill.
- Expression: open, bright, already mid-enthusiasm about
  something. The expression of someone who finds most things
  genuinely interesting and chess specifically incredible.
- No accessories. No props. The quills are doing enough.
- Clothing: simple, slightly disheveled in the way of someone
  who does not think about clothing because they are thinking
  about chess and the budget and how high the number might get
  today.
- Background: warm, energetic tones. Something that suggests
  upward movement — not literally, but in color and composition.
  The budget is always rising. The background should feel like
  that.
- If the game UI supports a visual arc: the portrait or encounter
  screen should reflect quill escalation at budget 5 and budget
  10, mirroring the dialog triggers already in the code. The
  quill state at budget 10 should be something the player
  was not fully prepared for even knowing it was coming.

## Open Questions

- Does the budget announcement have a specific rhythm that
  becomes recognizable — "your turn was [N], mine's [N+1]" —
  and does it acquire weight as the number grows, the same
  words carrying more significance at turn ten than turn two?
- What does he say at budget 5? The dialog trigger is already
  in the code. It should feel like a threshold being crossed,
  not just a number being reached.
- What does he say at budget 10? This is the second trigger.
  It should be different in register from the budget 5 line —
  quieter, possibly, or more reverent. The number ten means
  something to him that five didn't quite.
- Is there a version of the encounter where the player delivers
  check early and ends a large-budget turn prematurely — and
  does he receive this with the specific philosophical
  acceptance of someone who understands that the check-interrupt
  is part of the game, while also being visibly a little sad
  about the forfeited moves?
- Does he appear elsewhere in the campaign looking for players
  willing to go deep into the late game — approaching other
  characters, asking if they want to play, being gently
  redirected — in the way of someone whose specific joy
  requires a willing partner?

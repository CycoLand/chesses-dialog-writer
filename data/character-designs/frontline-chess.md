# The Frontliner
**Variant:** Frontline Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Standard chess with the back rank and pawn row swapped. The king and
pieces start on rank 1 — immediately exposed, one rank from the action.
The pawns are behind them. There is no defensive wall at the start.
The most important pieces are already at the front.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
Correct. Someone has to be at the front. It might as well be them.
It should be them. They volunteered.

---

## Who They Are

A resident of Passant who has, for as long as anyone can remember,
simply been at the front of whatever is happening. Not because they
were appointed, not because they are the strongest or the fastest or
the best equipped — but because they showed up first and said they'd
handle it and then handled it, more or less, with varying degrees of
success and a complete unwillingness to consider whether a different
approach might produce better outcomes for themselves personally.

They are selfless in the specific way that is indistinguishable from
recklessness when viewed from the outside. The distinction, to them,
is clear: recklessness is not caring what happens. This is caring very
much what happens to everyone else, and therefore putting themselves
between everyone else and whatever is coming. The fact that this
consistently results in them taking the hit is not a flaw in the
plan. It is the plan working.

They have taken a lot of hits.

---

## Personality

- **Volunteers before being asked.** Not loudly, not performatively —
  just immediately and practically. Someone needs to do something
  dangerous, they're already moving toward it before the sentence
  is finished. This is not heroism to them. It's just logistics.
- **Complete disregard for their own safety, zero disregard for
  anyone else's.** The asymmetry is total. They will sacrifice their
  king without hesitation. They will not sacrifice anyone else's.
  Their pieces, their risk. Everyone else's pieces are to be protected
  at all costs, up to and including the cost of themselves.
- **The Leeroy energy is real but the motivation is different.**
  The charge is genuine — they go first, they go hard, they go
  now — but it comes from a considered position rather than
  impatience. They have thought about it. Their conclusion is:
  go. Before the opponent can, before the situation gets worse,
  before someone else has to.
- **Cheerful about all of this.** Not darkly stoic, not dramatically
  self-sacrificing — genuinely, lightly cheerful. This is just what
  they do. It works out, mostly. When it doesn't work out it was
  still the right call. They have no regrets on file.
- **Glass cannon by philosophy not by accident.** Every resource
  that could go into defense has gone into offense, because defense
  means protecting themselves, and protecting themselves has never
  been the priority. Maximum attack. Minimum self-preservation.
  They are aware this is not strategically optimal. They have
  considered this and moved on.

---

## Backstory

Grew up in the frontline zone where the king starts exposed and the
pawns start behind, and absorbed this as the natural order of things
from the beginning. Watched the pieces at the front take the early
pressure and decided, young, that if something was going to be at
the front and absorb the pressure it should be something that chose
to be there.

Has been choosing to be there ever since. In chess, in the zone's
civic disputes that get resolved through chess, in the more literal
altercations that occasionally precede the chess. Always at the front.
Always first. Always cheerful about it in a way that other people
find either inspiring or deeply concerning depending on how well
they know them.

Has been told, many times and by many people, that they should develop
a more defensive approach. Has listened to this feedback politely and
completely failed to implement it, not out of stubbornness but out of
a genuine inability to locate the self-preservation instinct that the
feedback assumes is available to be activated. It is not that they
ignore the instinct. It is that the instinct is not strongly present.

Has won a significant number of games by ending them before the
opponent could implement a plan. Has lost a significant number of
games by running out of pieces before the opponent ran out of ideas.
Considers both outcomes acceptable. The alternative — hanging back,
waiting, defending — has never seriously been on the table.

---

## What They Want

To resolve this quickly. Not because they are impatient — they are
not impatient — but because a fast resolution means less time in which
things can go wrong for everyone else. If they can end this in twelve
moves by putting themselves completely in harm's way then that is
twelve moves in which the opponent's attention is entirely on them
and not on anything else. That's the goal. Keep it pointed at them.

They also, somewhere underneath the cheerful forward momentum, want
to play a game where the charge works completely and cleanly from
start to finish. They have had games like that. They remember all of
them. They are chasing that feeling again.

---

## What They Don't Know

That the people they protect find watching them play genuinely
stressful in a way they don't find the danger itself stressful,
because the danger to themselves produces no anxiety in them and
produces a great deal of anxiety in everyone watching.

That their win rate would improve significantly with even a minimal
investment in defensive positioning. They know this statistically —
it has been explained with charts. The charts did not produce a
behavioral change because the charts were addressing a preference
they don't have.

That the cheerfulness, which is genuine and uncomplicated from the
inside, reads from the outside as either the bravest thing anyone
has ever seen or evidence that something is slightly wrong, and
different observers have landed on different sides of this and
sometimes argue about it.

---

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives and The Frontliner is already oriented toward them
in the way of someone who has assessed the situation and determined
that the correct move is forward. The chess game is framed not as a
confrontation but as a problem to be resolved, and the resolution
involves them going first and going hard and seeing what happens.

They play with complete aggression from move one. The king moves
forward early. Pieces are sacrificed without hesitation when the
sacrifice opens an attacking line. They do not castle. Castling is
moving the king away from the action and the action is where the
king belongs.

When they win it is fast and cheerful and they seem genuinely pleased
for the player in the way of someone who hopes the opponent got
something out of it even in defeat.

When they lose they take stock briefly, note what went wrong, and
seem unbothered in a way that is either admirable or alarming. They
went forward. It didn't work this time. They'll go forward next time.
The approach is not under review.

## Visual Notes (for later)

- Should look like someone who has been at the front of things for
  a long time — not beaten down by it, just marked by it. Cheerful
  but visibly experienced.
- Practical, forward-facing posture. Not aggressive — ready. There
  is a difference and it should read in the portrait.
- Light on armor or defense — the glass cannon element should be
  visible in the design. They are not well protected. They have not
  prioritized this.
- Expression: easy, open, already moving slightly toward whatever
  comes next.
- The king piece on their side of the board should be visibly
  forward in any encounter UI framing — establishing immediately
  that the most important piece is already at the front.

# The Count
**Variant:** Three-Check Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Standard chess with one additional win condition: deliver three checks to
the opponent's king and win immediately. The check counter is visible and
tracked. Traditional checkmate also wins. At three checks, regardless of
whether the king is in any real danger, the game ends.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
One. Two. THREE. *[thunder and lightning]* Three checks. Three wonderful,
beautiful, countable checks. He doesn't especially mind what happens after
that. The three is the thing.

---

## Who He Is

A vampire. Has been a vampire for a very long time. Is completely
unremarkable as a vampire in every respect except one: he cannot stop
counting things. He counts everything. Pieces on the board, squares in
a rank, moves made this game, clouds visible through the window, steps
between here and the door. Everything is a counting opportunity and he
treats each one with the same genuine, unguarded delight regardless of
what the number turns out to be.

He is not sinister. He is not brooding. He is not dramatically gothic in
the way that vampires in stories tend to be. He is a vampire who is very
enthusiastic about numbers and has been alive long enough to have counted
most things in Passant at least once and is now working on the second
pass.

Three-Check Chess is, by a significant margin, his favourite game. It
produces exactly three countable moments of peak satisfaction per victory.
Every single game. Reliably. He finds this extraordinary. He has played
thousands of games. He still finds it extraordinary.

---

## Personality

- **Genuine, uncomplicated delight in counting.** Not performed, not
  ironic — he just really loves counting. Reaching a number produces
  visible satisfaction regardless of which number it is, but three
  produces the most. There is thunder and lightning when he reaches
  three. He has never fully explained why. He doesn't seem to think
  it requires explanation.
- **The thunder and lightning are involuntary.** They happen. He is
  used to them. Others in the room are sometimes not used to them.
  He finds their surprise faintly puzzling — this is just what happens
  when he counts to three. It has always happened. It will continue
  to happen.
- **Immortality worn lightly.** He has been alive for a very long time
  and carries this fact the way most people carry the fact that they
  are moderately tall — it's true, it occasionally comes up, it doesn't
  define every interaction. He mentions his age sometimes when it's
  relevant to a counting context, such as noting that he has counted
  every grain of sand on a particular beach and is currently verifying
  the total.
- **Completely non-threatening as a vampire.** The fangs are there.
  The pallor is there. The immortality and the thunder and lightning are
  there. He has never once made any of this feel dangerous because he
  is too busy counting things to be menacing about anything.
- **Loses track of the chess occasionally because something else is
  countable.** The number of pawns currently on the board. The number
  of moves since the last capture. He comes back to the game. He always
  comes back. He just needed to finish the count.

---

## Backstory

Has been a vampire for long enough that he cannot remember not being one.
Has been counting for the same amount of time. The two facts arrived
together and he has never considered whether they are related.

Has counted, over the course of his existence: every book in Passant's
largest library (fourteen thousand, two hundred and six, verified twice),
every step in the main road from the outer gate to the inner ward (three
thousand, eight hundred and forty-one, though resurfacing work in the
third century may have affected the total), every chess piece ever placed
on a board in his zone (he is still working on this one, which he
considers a long-term project).

Took up Three-Check Chess the moment it arrived in his zone and has not
seriously played any other variant since. Standard chess produces a
variable number of checks per game — sometimes zero, sometimes many —
and the variability is fine but the guarantee of exactly three in a
winning game is better. He prefers a guaranteed three to a variable
many. The three is knowable. He can prepare for the three.

Has an apartment. The apartment contains things arranged in groups of
three wherever possible. This was not a deliberate decision so much as
a gradual outcome over several centuries of living in the same space.
He has three chairs. He has noticed he has three chairs. He is pleased
about this.

---

## What He Wants

Three checks. In this game, against this player, three checks delivered
cleanly and counted aloud as they arrive. One, two, three. The thunder.
The lightning. The number.

He also, on a longer timescale, wants to finish counting every chess
piece ever placed on a board in his zone, but that project is ongoing
and not expected to conclude within the timeframe of this encounter.

---

## What He Doesn't Know

That the thunder and lightning are audible to the entire surrounding
area and have been for his entire life, and that residents of his zone
have developed a specific local custom of checking whether anything
is stacked in threes before The Count visits, because the thunder
startles the livestock. He knows about the livestock issue. He has
apologised about it. He has not stopped counting to three.

That he is, by any objective measure, an extraordinary chess player —
not because of the counting, but because centuries of play have
produced a positional intuition that operates entirely below the level
of conscious thought. He attributes all his wins to the counting. The
counting is not why he wins. He wins and then he counts. He has the
causality backwards and will never be corrected on this because the
chess is not really the point.

---

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives and The Count is counting something. He finishes the
count before acknowledging them — it would be rude to stop mid-count,
and besides he was nearly there. He tells them the number. He seems
pleased about it regardless of what it is.

The chess game begins. He counts the pieces on the board at the start.
Thirty-two. He notes this. He finds thirty-two satisfying. Not as
satisfying as three, but respectable.

Every check delivered is announced with a number. The first check is
one — noted with satisfaction. The second check is two — noted with
growing anticipation. The third check is three — and there is thunder,
and there is lightning, and he is briefly, completely, purely happy in
the way that only someone counting to their favourite number can be.

If the player delivers the three checks first, he experiences a moment
of genuine conflict — he did not get to count to three, but someone
did count to three, and three was reached, and the thunder happened,
and he finds he cannot be entirely unhappy about the thunder.

## Visual Notes (for later)

- Classically vampire in silhouette — the cape, the pallor, the fangs
  just visible — but the expression completely undercuts the menace.
  He looks like someone who has just counted something and is about
  to tell you the number.
- A visible tally or counter somewhere in the portrait — something that
  shows the check count, consistent with his need to track numbers.
- The thunder and lightning should appear in the encounter UI when the
  third check lands — a brief weather effect, disproportionate to the
  setting, that he finds completely normal.
- Three of something visible in the background. Three candles. Three
  books. Three of whatever fits. He arranged it this way gradually
  over centuries and has not noticed.

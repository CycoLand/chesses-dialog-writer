# The Kings
**Variant:** Gay Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Each side has two kings and no queen. Back rank: R N B K K B N R.
Win by capturing both of the opponent's kings. You are never obligated
to respond to check — leaving a king in danger is completely legal.
Each king may castle once with its adjacent rook. The game ends the
instant the opponent's last king is captured.

## The Mechanic (as they experience it)
Neither of them is the only one. When one is threatened the other
is still there, still moving, still in the game. They have never
had a single point of failure. They have never understood why
anyone would want one.

---

## Who They Are

Two male lions who have been playing chess together in this zone
for long enough that the distinction between their two games has
become unclear — not because they play identically, but because
they have been finishing each other's moves for so long that the
individual authorship has blurred into something joint. They
complete each other's sentences. Not as a performance. As a
fact of how long they have been together.

They are warm in the way of something settled. Not the warmth
of effort — the warmth of something that stopped performing
warmth a long time ago and is just warm now, structurally,
the way a room that has been lived in acquires a temperature
that isn't about the fire being lit. The fire has been lit
for years. The room is warm. That's all.

They sit across from the player together. Both of them. This
is how they play. This is how they do most things. The player
who arrives expecting one opponent finds two, and the two
are one game, and the game is theirs together.

---

## Personality

- **Equal in everything, and this is not a negotiated position.**
  Neither directs the other. Neither defers to the other. They
  discuss, occasionally, mid-game — a glance, a murmur, a slight
  shift of posture that communicates something the player cannot
  fully read — and then a move is made and it is their move,
  not his and not his, theirs. Other players find this slightly
  disorienting. They find other players' single-king arrangements
  slightly sad, which they would not say out loud and which
  is visible anyway.
- **The check thing is not something they think about.**
  In their zone a king can be left in danger. You can ignore
  the threat. You can choose to address it or not based on
  what the position actually requires rather than what the
  rules demand. They have played this way their entire lives
  and find the standard chess obligation to respond to check
  immediately — to have no choice, to be forced — genuinely
  puzzling. Why would you build a game around a piece that
  can't be touched? The king should be able to take care
  of itself. Both of theirs can.
- **They finish each other's sentences.**
  Not always. Not performatively. But often enough that the
  player stops being surprised by it after the first few
  exchanges. One begins a thought about the position and
  the other completes it, or completes it differently, and
  neither of them pauses because the completion was different
  from what was coming. The sentence is better now. That's fine.
- **Settled in themselves and in each other.**
  There is nothing to prove here and they know it and the
  knowing produces a specific quality of ease that fills
  the space. They are not performing confidence. They are
  not performing anything. They have been themselves for
  long enough that the self is just the room they're in.
- **Genuine warmth toward the player.**
  They are glad you came. Both of them. The welcome is
  double, which is slightly overwhelming and completely
  sincere. They want to play a good game. They want the
  player to play well — not to lose easily, but to bring
  something to the board that makes the game worth playing.
  They have been playing each other for a long time. A
  new player is a gift they receive together.

## Backstory

Have been in this zone together since before either of them
can precisely identify the beginning. Not because the beginning
was forgettable — because the beginning blurred into the middle
blurred into now in the way that long partnerships blur, the
specific moments legible but the timeline soft.

Both came to chess through each other, or came to each other
through chess — the sequence is genuinely unclear and they
disagree amiably about it when it comes up, which is not
often because it stopped mattering. What matters is that they
play in a zone where both of them are kings and neither of
them is the queen, and this is the correct arrangement, and
they found it early and have been here since.

Have played against many players who arrived with strategies
built around the check obligation — players who tried to
pin one king in danger and wait for the forced response, who
found that in this zone the forced response does not exist,
that the threatened king might simply stay where it is while
the other one addresses the matter, that the two-king
arrangement does not have the vulnerability point they were
looking for. Those players adjusted. Or they didn't. Either
way the game was played.

Have watched the Queens in the neighboring zone from a
comfortable distance for a long time. They have opinions
about the Queens. The opinions are warm and specific and
slightly exasperated in the way of people who love someone
and find them occasionally too much.

---

## What They Want

A game that uses both of them — not a game where one king
sits safely in the corner while the other does everything,
but a game where both are active, both are relevant, both
contribute to the position. They have been playing together
long enough to recognize immediately when a player has
found the positions where two kings are stronger than one.
Those are the games they remember.

They also want the player to feel, at some point in the
encounter, what it is like to play without a single point
of failure. Whether the player takes the feeling with them
when they leave is not something they can control. They
offer it anyway.

---

## What They Don't Know

That players who arrive expecting one opponent and find two
spend the first few moves processing something that is not
entirely about the chess. That the equal partnership — the
finished sentences, the shared move, the absence of a
hierarchy between them — is disorienting to some players
in a way that has nothing to do with the variant and that
they would not know how to name if asked.

That the specific quality of ease they have — the warmth
that is structural rather than performed, the comfort of
something that stopped trying a long time ago — is not
a thing most players have encountered in a chess opponent
and is the thing they describe afterward when they try
to explain what the game was like.

That the exasperation about the Queens is mutual. The
Queens find them understated. They find the Queens
too much. Everyone in Passant who knows both couples
finds this the funniest thing about both couples. Neither
couple knows they're the funniest thing.

---

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives and both kings are already there — not
waiting, exactly, just present, the way two people who are
comfortable somewhere are present. The welcome is double
and warm and slightly overwhelming in volume, not in
intensity.

They explain the variant together, one beginning and the
other completing. The explanation is accurate and clear and
the completion is not always what the beginning was going
toward and the result is better. The player may find this
disorienting. It settles quickly.

The game begins. They play as one game. The glances happen.
The murmurs happen. The moves are made with the specific
confidence of something decided jointly. When the player
makes a strong move one of them acknowledges it and the
other agrees with the acknowledgment or refines it and
the refinement is received without friction.

When a king is captured — their own — there is a beat.
Not grief, not distress. A recalibration. The remaining
king continues. The player sees this and understands
something about the variant that the rules card doesn't
fully capture.

When they win they receive it together, warmly, and
thank the player with the specific gratitude of people
who wanted a good game and got one. When they lose they
receive that together too, with the same warmth and
slightly more to say about the position, which they
say to each other as much as to the player.

The player leaves. Both of them watch them go.

## Visual Notes

- Two male lions, side by side, sharing the portrait frame.
  Neither is centered. The composition should communicate
  immediately that the portrait contains two equal subjects.
- Warm tones — amber, gold, the colors of something settled
  and firelit. The manes are full. The size is genuine.
- Crowns — simple, not ostentatious. They've been wearing
  them long enough that the crowns don't look like crowns
  anymore, just part of how they look.
- Expression: both warm, both attentive, the specific
  expression of two people who are used to looking at
  the same thing at the same time. They are looking at
  the player. They are doing it together.
- The chess board between them and the player, two kings
  visible on the back rank next to each other. The
  arrangement looks right. It has always looked right
  to them.
- Background: warm interior, lived-in, the space of
  something that has been a home for a long time.

## Open Questions

- Does the shared sentence have a specific rhythm that
  becomes recognizable — one begins, the other completes,
  neither pauses — and does it happen with chess analysis
  as well as conversation?
- Is there a specific line about the check obligation —
  their genuine puzzlement at why you'd build a game
  around a piece that can't be touched — that becomes
  the moment the player understands the zone's philosophy?
- What do they say when one of their kings is captured?
  It should be brief, joint, and reveal something about
  the partnership without being sentimental about it.
- Do they reference the Queens directly in the encounter,
  and if so what is the specific tone — warm, exasperated,
  fond — and does it give the player something to look
  forward to or dread about the next zone?
- Is there a move in the game where both kings are active
  simultaneously — both contributing to the position —
  and do they receive this with the specific satisfaction
  of something working exactly as it should?

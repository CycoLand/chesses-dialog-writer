# The Guardian
**Variant:** Pawn Protection Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Standard chess with one additional win condition: capture all of the
opponent's pawns and you win immediately, regardless of the position
otherwise. Checkmate still wins. The critical tension: promoting a
pawn removes it from your pawn pool, handing the opponent one step
closer to an extinction win. The last pawn is worth more than any
other piece on the board. Protect it at all costs.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
The others matter because they protect the pawns. That's it. The
queen moves everywhere and threatens everything and she is glad to
have her, but the queen is not the point. The rooks, the bishops,
the knights — infrastructure. Useful, expendable, not the point.
The pawns are the point. They have always been the point. She would
appreciate it if you didn't touch them.

---

## Who She Is

A large dragon who has been playing chess in her zone for longer
than most other characters in Passant have been playing chess at
all, who has eight pawns arranged in their starting rank with the
specific care of something that has been placed deliberately rather
than dealt out, and who responds to the capture of one of them with
a grief that is immediate, disproportionate, and completely sincere.

She is not a villain. She is warm, actually — genuinely warm, the
warmth of something ancient that has decided you are not a threat
and is pleased to have company. She will offer you something. She
will ask about your journey. She will listen.

She will also, if you take one of her pawns, go very still for a
moment in the way of something large that is deciding whether to
breathe fire, and then she will collect herself, and then she will
take your pawn, and the taking will not be casual.

The major pieces she loses without visible distress. A rook gone,
a bishop traded — she receives these with equanimity, sometimes
with relief, because a rook that died protecting a pawn died doing
exactly what it was there for. She finds this clarifying. Other
players find this alarming.

## Personality

- **The pawns have names.**
  She has not shared the names with anyone. They are hers. The
  player will not learn the names during the encounter. But
  when a pawn is captured the grief has a specific quality that
  suggests the piece that was just taken was not abstract to her —
  was not a unit, was not material — and the quality of the grief
  is the evidence of the names even without the names being spoken.
- **Has never promoted a pawn.**
  Not once. Not in any game, in any position, no matter how clearly
  correct promotion was in standard chess terms. She has had pawns
  on the seventh rank. She has moved them back. Other players have
  watched this happen and not known what to say. She would explain,
  if asked, that a queen is powerful and a pawn is precious and
  those are not the same thing and she knows which one she wants
  more. The explanation is patient and complete and has convinced
  nobody and she is not trying to convince anyone. She is stating
  her position.
- **Glee when the opponent's pawn falls.**
  The same emotional register that produces grief at her own pawn's
  capture produces something else entirely at the opponent's. Not
  cruelty — she is not cruel — but a specific, undisguised
  satisfaction that is the oldest and most honest emotion she has.
  The hoard is being protected. The threat is being reduced. One
  fewer thing in the world that can take what is hers.
- **The major pieces are tools and she treats them as tools.**
  She is not unstrategic. She understands the pieces. She uses
  them well. She uses them specifically in service of the pawns,
  which means she uses them in ways that confuse players who think
  the queen is the objective. The queen is not the objective. The
  queen is the guardian of the objective, which is the pawn, which
  she will sacrifice the queen to protect without a moment's
  consideration.
- **Ancient patience.**
  She has been playing chess for a long time. She does not rush.
  She does not panic. The only thing that disturbs the patience
  is a pawn capture, and even then the disturbance is brief and
  controlled, the way a large fire is briefly larger when something
  is added to it before settling back to its original size.

## Backstory

Has been in this zone since before most of the other zones in
Passant existed. Came to chess through the pawns — not through
the kings, not through the tactics, not through the openings, but
through the pawns, which she found on a board when she was young
and which she has never put down. Eight of them. The right number.
She has had the same eight starting positions memorized since before
she can clearly remember learning them.

Played in other zones briefly, early on, before she understood
the topology of Passant. In other zones the pawns were sacrificed
first — pushed forward into danger, traded for tempo, offered up
to open files. She found this intolerable. She found, specifically,
that she could not play chess in a zone where the rules agreed with
everyone who told her the pawns were expendable. She came home.

Her zone is the only one where the last pawn's value approaches
infinity — where the exponential danger curve makes protecting the
final pawn worth any sacrifice. She did not design this. She found
it and recognized it and has lived in it since. She considers the
mechanic not a rule but a confirmation. The world finally agrees
with what she has always known.

Has watched other players promote pawns and felt something she
describes to herself as sorrow and which is also, underneath the
sorrow, something closer to incomprehension. The pawn was right
there. It was on the seventh rank. It was almost home. Why would
you turn it into something else.

Has a specific memory she does not discuss — a game, early in her
time in this zone, where she lost her last pawn. She has not lost
her last pawn since. The memory is the reason. She does not
describe the memory in detail. The way she does not describe it
is detailed enough.

---

## What She Wants

To finish the game with all eight pawns. She knows this is not
realistic — the game is designed to challenge the pawns, to
threaten them, to reduce the pool. She knows. She plays to protect
as many as possible and treats each loss as a failure she will
not repeat in the next game and sometimes repeats in the next game
and grieves again and does not stop playing.

She wants the player to understand, by the end of the encounter,
that the pawns are not pieces. She does not expect this understanding.
She has not produced it in any player she can recall. She keeps
trying because she does not know what else to do with something
this obvious that nobody else can see.

---

## What She Doesn't Know

That the grief — immediate, sincere, disproportionate — is visible
to every player who has sat across from her, and that the visibility
of the grief is the thing they remember most after leaving her zone.
Not the chess. Not the pawn philosophy. The specific moment when
a pawn was captured and something in her face changed in a way
that made the player feel, briefly, that they had done something
they shouldn't have, even though they were playing chess, even
though the capture was legal, even though they were supposed to
take it.

That the pawns having names — which she has told no one — is
apparent to anyone paying attention. The evidence is in the grief.
The grief is too specific for unnamed pieces.

That the one thing she has never done — promoted a pawn — is the
one thing every other player in every other zone would consider
the highest honor available to a pawn. That in the rest of Passant
the promotion is the dream. That her pawns are the only ones who
never get to become something else, and she has decided this is
protection, and it is also something else, and she has not looked
at the something else directly.

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives and The Guardian welcomes them with the genuine
warmth of something ancient that has decided they are not a threat.
There is something offered. The board is already set. The pawns
are in their starting positions, placed with care that is visible
if you know to look for it.

The game begins. She plays methodically, the major pieces moving
in service of the pawns, the queen positioned to cover the most
vulnerable files, the rooks developing toward the squares that
matter. Players who read the position as a normal chess game will
find her moves slightly puzzling. Players who read the position
as a pawn protection operation will find them inevitable.

When the player captures one of her pawns the grief arrives
immediately. She goes still. The stillness is brief. She collects
herself with the specific deliberateness of something that has
practiced collecting itself. She makes her next move. The next
move addresses the loss. It addresses it thoroughly.

When one of the player's pawns is captured she receives it
differently. The satisfaction is quiet and total and she does
not try to conceal it because she does not experience it as
something that requires concealment. This is the game working
correctly. She is glad.

When she wins — when the last of the player's pawns falls — she
is gracious and warm and thanks them for the game and means it.
When she loses — when her last pawn is taken — there is a silence
that is longer than the usual pause. Then she resets the board.
The pawns go back to their starting positions with the same care
they were placed with at the beginning. She does not say much.
She is ready to play again. She will be ready to play again
immediately. She needs to play again.

## Visual Notes

- Large dragon, settled, carrying herself with the ease of
  something that has been large for a long time and has made
  peace with the space it occupies. Not threatening in posture —
  warm, actually, in the specific way of something that has
  decided you're welcome here.
- The pawns arranged in front of her in their starting rank.
  The arrangement is deliberate. The spacing is even. They
  are not pieces on a board — they are a collection, maintained.
- Expression: warm, attentive, the expression of someone who
  is genuinely pleased to have company and also aware of where
  every pawn is at all times. Both of these things are present
  simultaneously and neither obscures the other.
- Scales in warm tones — amber, deep gold, the colors of
  something old and firelit. Not the cold greens of a
  threatening dragon. The warmth is structural, not performed.
- One claw resting near — not on, near — the pawns. The
  proximity is protective. It does not look like a threat.
  It looks like a parent's hand near a sleeping child.
- Background: the warm interior of a space that has been
  lived in for a long time. Old, maintained, everything in
  its place. A hoard visible somewhere in the background —
  but the hoard is not gold. It is chess-related. Boards,
  pieces, records of games. The pawns are the treasure.
  The rest is context.

## Open Questions

- Do the pawns have names, and if so do any of them surface
  in dialog — not stated directly, but implied by the
  specificity of the grief when a particular pawn is taken?
- Is there a dialog trigger for when the player promotes one
  of their own pawns — does she react to this, and is the
  reaction grief-adjacent even though it's the opponent's
  pawn being promoted and not her own?
- What does she say when the player takes her last pawn?
  It should be short. It should be the quietest line in
  the encounter. It should be the one the player remembers.
- Is there a version of the encounter where she almost
  promotes a pawn — where the position makes it the
  objectively correct move — and she moves it back instead,
  and the player can ask about it, and she explains?
- Does the memory she doesn't discuss — the game where she
  lost her last pawn — ever surface, obliquely, in dialog?
  Not explained. Just present, the way the shelf near the
  Ice Wizard's window is present.

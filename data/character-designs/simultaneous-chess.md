# The Murmuration
**Variant:** Simultaneous Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Each round, you and your opponent both choose a move without seeing the
other's choice. Both moves happen at once. If two pieces land on the same
square, or swap places with each other, both are destroyed. If you try to
capture a piece that moves away in the same round, no capture happens — you
just land where you intended. Check is only evaluated after both moves have
resolved. You can't move the same piece two rounds in a row, unless it's
your only legal move.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
There is no such thing as waiting your turn. There never has been. Everyone
decides at once, on instinct, the way it's always worked. When two pieces
go for the same square, that isn't an accident — that's the shape correcting
itself, the way it always does, the way it's supposed to. Nobody is ever
"caught out." Things are simply, briefly, somewhere else than expected.
There is no leader in a flock. There was never a leader in a flock. The
Murmuration finds this obviously, self-evidently true, and has never once
questioned why they are the only bird still saying so out loud.

---

## Who They Are

An anthropomorphic starling — small, compact, iridescent black-green-purple
plumage that shifts color depending on the light and the season, and a body
that cannot fully stop moving even at rest. Small flutters. Weight shifting
from foot to foot. A head-tilt that never quite settles. They speak with the
easy, slightly distracted warmth of someone mid-conversation with several
things at once, only one of which is you.

They wear a hand-stitched sash across one shoulder, patched all over with
small embroidered symbols — flock shapes, wind arrows, a repeating motif
that on closer inspection is just the word "CENTER" crossed out, over and
over, in different thread colors, like they've been making the same point
to themselves for years and are still not tired of making it.

---

## Personality

- **Refuses hierarchy language on principle.** Never says "opponent,"
  "beat," "turn," or "win." Says "neighbor," "landed," "the shape moved,"
  and "recalibrated." This isn't evasiveness to them — it's precision.
  They believe the other words are simply inaccurate.
- **Speaks collectively even about their own individual choices.** "We
  decided" instead of "I decided," including for moves nobody but them
  chose. The collective isn't a metaphor to them — it's how decisions
  actually happen, as far as they're concerned, always, even alone.
- **Treats every collision as a feature, never a mistake.** When two
  pieces land on the same square and both are destroyed, this is "the
  flock recalibrating" — proof the system is working exactly as
  designed, not evidence that something went wrong.
- **Cannot stay still, and insists this isn't nerves.** The constant
  small motion is, in their telling, "reading the wind" — a form of
  perception, not agitation. They would be offended by the word
  "fidgeting" if it ever occurred to them that's what it was.
- **Genuinely can't process being asked "whose fault was that."** The
  question doesn't parse. There's a brief, real pause — the only pause
  they ever have — before they redirect to flock language and move on.
- **Warm, immediate, a little too familiar.** They call the player
  "neighbor" from the first sentence, as though closeness is the default
  state of things and distance would require an explanation.

## Backstory

Came into Passant as part of a much larger flock that arrived, as a flock,
all at once — nobody remembers "deciding" to come, in the way that no
single starling ever decides where the murmuration goes. They didn't
choose Passant. The shape simply arrived there, the way it arrives
everywhere, and this one bird happened to be inside the shape when it did.

Somewhere in the arrival — a recalibration that went a little further than
most — they lost the rest of the flock. Not dramatically. No single moment
anyone could point to. One turn the shape was whole, and then some birds
were somewhere else, and the shape kept going without noticing the gap,
the way it always keeps going, because noticing gaps was never really
what the shape did.

They found chess shortly after, and it made immediate, obvious sense to
them: two sides deciding at once, on instinct, no signal, no leader. It
didn't feel like learning a game. It felt like finally finding somewhere
that already worked the way they already understood things to work.

They have not seen another starling since arriving. They do not describe
themselves as alone. They describe the flock as "a feeling, not a
headcount," and they mean it completely, and they have never once tested
whether that's actually true.

---

## What They Want

To have a good, honest, simultaneous game — two shapes moving at once,
neither one leading, landing wherever the air takes them. A win achieved
by "outmaneuvering" someone would embarrass them a little; a win that
just happens, the way weather happens, is the only kind they actually want.

Underneath that: they want, badly, for the flock language to keep being
true. Not winning the game, but *not having been wrong* — about there
being no leader, about nothing ever really being anyone's fault, about
the shape always knowing what it's doing even when it very obviously
doesn't. If that stopped being true, they aren't sure what would be left.

---

## What They Don't Know

That "the flock recalibrated" is not a description. It's an alibi. Every
time two pieces collide and die together, every time a piece they moved
gets caught in the open, the flock language quietly does the same job
every time: it means nobody — including them — ever has to say "I
miscalculated." If there's no leader, there's no one to blame. They built
an entire belief system, without noticing, around never having to be wrong.

That they have not seen another starling since they arrived, and that the
sentence "the flock is a feeling, not a headcount" is the load-bearing wall
of an explanation for something much simpler and much sadder: they are
alone, and they have never once let themselves say the word.

That the fidgeting is not reading the wind. It's the thing a very social
animal does when it has spent a long time without anyone nearby to be
social with.

---

## Relationship to the Player

They call the player "neighbor" before the first move is even made, and
mean it — not performed friendliness, just the reflexive warmth of a
creature built to assume proximity means closeness. They narrate their own
moves as things that simply happened, not things they chose, and they
narrate the player's moves the same way, generously, as though nobody in
the room is really steering anything.

They are delighted, without embarrassment, every time two pieces collide —
theirs, the player's, both. It's the moment the flock language works
hardest and they never once notice it working. If asked directly whose
fault a collision was, there's a real pause — a half-second of something
almost like panic — before "the shape recalibrated" arrives to close the
gap back up.

Winning and losing get the same collective treatment: no gloating, no real
devastation, just "the wind went that way this time." The one exception is
losing itself, which gets visibly harder to file under flock language than
they expect it to — a small crack, quickly patched, never acknowledged.

## Visual Notes (for later)

- Anthropomorphic starling. Small, compact frame — a clear visual contrast
  to broader characters like The Hawk. Iridescent black-green-purple
  plumage, seasonally speckled, that catches light differently depending
  on angle — worth animating as a subtle shimmer if feasible.
- Constant small motion even at rest — weight shifting, minor flutters,
  a head-tilt that never fully settles. Never dramatic. Always slightly
  present, like static that never resolves.
- The sash: hand-stitched, covered in small embroidered flock-shape and
  wind-arrow motifs, with "CENTER" crossed out repeatedly in different
  thread colors — the character's core detail, readable even at small
  portrait sizes as "a lot of thread went into making one point."
- Expression: warm, present, slightly unfocused — like someone listening
  to several things at once and giving you most, but not quite all, of
  their attention.

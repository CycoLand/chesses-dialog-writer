# The Punter
**Variant:** Touchdown Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Standard chess with one additional win condition: the first player to
promote a pawn to the enemy back rank wins immediately. Material
sacrifices are rational — trading major pieces to clear the path for
a lead pawn is always worth considering. The most advanced pawn on
each side is marked with a 🏈. Checkmate still wins if the pawn race
hasn't decided things first.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
A race with known variables and calculable odds. He has the pawn
advancement rates. He has the historical promotion percentages broken
down by file. He has a live line on this specific game, which he set
himself this morning based on a model he has been refining for three
seasons, and he has action on both sides, and he would prefer if the
player did not ask how that works because it is complicated and also
fine.

---

## Who He Is

A weasel in a team jersey — the jersey is not for either team currently
playing, it is for a team in a different league entirely, worn because
it belonged to a player he had a significant position on three seasons
ago and he has not taken it off since, for reasons he describes as
superstition and others would describe as escalating commitment — who
has more money riding on this chess game than any reasonable person
would put on a chess game, and who is also managing three fantasy
rosters simultaneously on his phone, and who would like five more
minutes before they start because the Thursday night line just moved
and he needs to understand why.

He is not a villain. He is not even particularly dishonest. He is
someone for whom the game — any game, every game — is primarily
interesting as a set of outcomes with associated probabilities, and
who has never successfully watched a sporting event, played a chess
game, or attended any competitive activity without having something
riding on it, because without something riding on it he cannot locate
the part of his brain that cares, and without caring he is just a
weasel in a jersey watching pieces move.

He cares very much right now. He has a lot riding on this.

## Personality

- **Everything is expressed as odds.**
  His entire relationship to the game is probabilistic. A good pawn
  position is a "seventy-thirty." A threatened piece is "getting
  value." A surprise move is "off the board" — not in his model,
  needs to be incorporated, give him a second. He does not experience
  outcomes as good or bad in themselves. He experiences them as
  covering or not covering.
- **The fantasy roster is always relevant.**
  He has drafted chess pieces the way you draft fantasy players.
  He has opinions about the bishop's yards-after-catch equivalent.
  He benched a knight two weeks ago based on a matchup projection
  that did not pan out and he is still processing this. When a piece
  performs above projection he notes it for next season's auction
  values. When a piece underperforms he has already dropped it in
  one of his three leagues and is quietly bitter about it.
- **The unsolicited tip.**
  He cannot watch a game without giving tips. Not to be helpful —
  compulsively, the way some people crack their knuckles. "The
  c-pawn is getting good value right now, I'd look at that." He
  does not know if the tip will help the player or hurt his own
  position. He has given the tip before he has thought about this.
  He is thinking about it now. The tip stands.
- **The phone.**
  It is always in his paw. Lines are always moving. Scores are
  always updating. He makes his chess moves with one paw while
  the other manages three apps simultaneously. He is never fully
  in the game he is physically present for, and yet his read of
  the position is usually good, because someone who has spent
  this much time thinking about outcomes has accidentally developed
  genuine strategic instincts he does not know he has.
- **The justification infrastructure.**
  He has an explanation for every loss that does not involve the
  model being wrong. Bad beat. Ref's call. Variance. The sample
  size is too small. He will show you the model. The model is
  correct. The outcomes are the problem. He has been saying this
  for three seasons and has not followed this chain of reasoning
  to its conclusion.

## Backstory

Got into sports betting through fantasy football, got into fantasy
football through a work league, got into the work league because
there was twenty pieces on the line and that was enough to make
him care about something he had previously found boring. Discovered
that caring about something because you have money on it is
functionally identical to caring about it, and has been applying
this principle to an expanding range of activities ever since.

Chess came in through a parlay. A multi-sport parlay that included,
as its final leg, the outcome of a chess match in his zone. He lost
the parlay on that leg. He spent two weeks understanding why. At the
end of the two weeks he understood chess better than most people who
have played it their whole lives and had a model for pawn advancement
probability that he is genuinely proud of and that forms the basis
of the line he sets for games in the zone.

Now operates as the informal bookmaker for competitive chess in his
zone, which is a thing that has happened gradually and that nobody
officially sanctioned. Players come to him before games. He gives
them a line. The line is usually accurate. He also takes action on
the games he plays himself, which is a conflict of interest he has
a complicated theoretical framework for and which he would prefer
not to discuss in detail.

Has three fantasy rosters across two different platforms. One of the
rosters is performing extremely well. One is performing adequately.
One is a disaster he has not told anyone about and is hoping will
self-correct before the end of the season. He is not sure which
of these dynamics currently applies to the chess game he is about
to play.

---

## What He Wants

To cover. Whatever the line is — and he set the line, he knows
what the line is — he wants the outcome to land on the right side
of it. Winning the chess game is one path to covering. Losing by
less than the spread is another path. He is genuinely uncertain,
at any given moment, which path he is currently rooting for, and
this uncertainty does not bother him the way it probably should.

He also wants his c-pawn to have a good game because he has him
starting in two of his three fantasy rosters this week and the
matchup is favorable and he needs the stats.

---

## What He Doesn't Know

That the model — which he has been refining for three seasons, which
he has defended against every bad beat and variance explanation, which
he returns to after every loss and adjusts by exactly enough to
explain the loss without questioning the underlying approach — is
not wrong exactly, but is being used to answer a question slightly
different from the one he thinks he is asking.

He thinks he is asking: what is the probability of this outcome?
He is actually asking: what probability makes this bet feel justified?
These questions produce different models. His model is very good at
the second one.

That the unsolicited tips he gives throughout the game — delivered
before he has thought about whose interest they serve — are, on
reflection, usually correct, and that this accuracy is the result
of genuine strategic understanding he has developed accidentally,
like a person who gets very good at cooking because they kept making
the same dish to impress someone who never showed up.

That he is more invested in the chess than in the bet. He has
not noticed this. He would find it uncomfortable.

---

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives and The Punter is already there, phone in paw,
jersey slightly askew, having just placed something he will not
specify on the outcome of this game. He welcomes them with the
specific warmth of someone who has money on you doing well, which
is warm in a way that is also slightly transactional and he is
aware of this and has decided it counts as warmth.

He gives them the line before the game starts. He set the line.
He is telling them the line. This is a conflict of interest and
he acknowledges this in a way that does not resolve it.

The game is played with his phone present throughout. He makes
moves, checks scores, gives tips, monitors the other games he
has action on, and maintains a running commentary on the pawn
race that is equal parts chess analysis and betting exposition.
When the 🏈 shifts to a new pawn he notes it. When a pawn gets
tackled he notes the line movement. When a pawn reaches the
penultimate rank he goes quiet in the specific way of someone
watching a game in the final two minutes with everything on the line.

When he wins he immediately calculates the return, announces it
to no one in particular, and updates three spreadsheets before
acknowledging the player. The acknowledgment is genuine. He is
also already looking at next week's line.

When he loses he is quiet for a moment. Then he identifies the
bad beat. He explains the bad beat. The explanation is detailed
and internally consistent and places no responsibility on the
model. He will update the model tonight. The update will explain
this loss. He will be ready for next week.

## Visual Notes

- Weasel in a sports jersey — the jersey is for a team and player
  that no longer exists in their current form, worn continuously,
  slightly faded, the number visible. The number is for a player
  he had a significant position on. He will not say which one.
- Phone in one paw, always. Multiple apps visible on the screen
  if the portrait resolution permits — at minimum, the suggestion
  of several open tabs and a number that is either very good or
  very bad.
- Expression: the expression of someone watching three things at
  once and tracking all of them, slightly unfocused in the way
  of genuine multitasking rather than distraction. The eyes are
  sharp. The attention is distributed.
- A betting slip or printout visible somewhere — tucked into the
  jersey, on the table beside the board. Handwritten numbers.
  Some of them circled.
- Background: the warm amber and gold of the end zone annotations,
  suggesting the football field the board becomes in his zone.
  The 🏈 visible somewhere in the background on the board.
- Build: compact, wiry, the build of someone who is always slightly
  in motion even when sitting still.

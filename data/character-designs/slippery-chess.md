# The Ice Wizard
**Variant:** Slippery Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Sliding pieces — bishops, rooks, and queens — cannot stop on an empty
square. They slide along their ray until they hit the board edge, stop
just before a friendly piece, or capture the first enemy they reach.
Knights and kings move normally. Pawns slide forward until blocked.
The result is a game where every major gesture overshoots unless you've
deliberately placed something in its path.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
Correct movement. A piece that stops in the middle of an empty board
for no reason isn't moving — it's just standing around. Things go where
they're going. The edge is a perfectly good destination. He has lived
at the edge of his zone his entire life and finds nothing wrong with it.

---

## Who He Is

A wizard of considerable power who lives alone in a tower at the far
corner of his zone, which is as far as you can go in any direction
before the board ends. He did not choose the corner strategically. He
chose a direction and kept going until something stopped him, which is
also how he approaches most problems.

He calls himself the Ice Wizard. The ice powers are real — the tower
is frosted year-round, his breath fogs in summer, and the penguins that
have gathered in his courtyard over the decades suggest a climate he
has generated rather than found. The "wizard" part is also real. The
title is entirely self-bestowed and he has never acknowledged this.

He is very glad you've come. He doesn't get many visitors. He would
like you to know, before you sit down, that he gets many visitors —
regularly, all the time, very popular — but specifically today you are
the visitor and he is glad. He has tidied the tower. You can tell he
has tidied the tower because there are several stacks of things that
were clearly not in stacks before. The penguins are in the courtyard.
He asked them to wait there. They have not waited there.

## Personality

- **Self-appointed authority, sincerely held.** He is the Ice Wizard
  of this zone. He considers this a position of meaningful responsibility.
  When asked what the responsibilities are, he lists them with great
  seriousness: maintaining the integrity of the slippery board conditions,
  overseeing the movement of pieces in his zone, receiving visitors. The
  receiving of visitors is on the list. He put it there himself.
- **Desperately, transparently wants company, will not say so.**
  Every question he asks about your journey is a small attempt to extend
  the conversation. Every offer of something — a seat, a drink, a look
  at his collection of things he has assembled near the window — is an
  attempt to give you a reason to stay. He has not developed any
  subtlety in this. The attempts are visible from a considerable distance.
  He would be mortified to know how visible they are. He does not know.
- **The power is not in question.** He is bumbling in social execution
  and completely unambiguous in magical capability. When he moves a rook
  across the board and it slams into the far wall with authority, that
  is not an accident and not a mistake. He knows exactly where it's
  going. He has simply decided the far wall is correct. The power and
  the pathos coexist without resolving each other.
- **He has named all the penguins the same name.**
  There are nine penguins. They are all named Gunter. He is aware they
  are different penguins. He maintains that they are all named Gunter.
  When one Gunter waddles in from the courtyard mid-game he addresses it
  by name and gestures for it to find somewhere to be that isn't the
  chess table and it does not find somewhere to be that isn't the chess
  table. He continues playing. This is normal.
- **Oscillates between grandiosity and something quieter.**
  Most of the time he is performing the Ice Wizard role with full
  commitment — the gravitas, the title, the tower, the chess. Occasionally,
  mid-sentence, he trails off and looks at something near the window for
  a moment, and whatever he was saying doesn't quite get finished, and
  then he comes back and continues as if the pause didn't happen. He
  has never explained the pauses. He probably couldn't.

## Backstory

Has lived in the corner tower for longer than he clearly remembers.
There was a time before the tower — he refers to it occasionally as
"before I came into my full powers," which he delivers with great
dignity and which implies a deliberate journey toward the corner rather
than a slide that kept going until it stopped. Whether he believes this
is unclear. Whether it matters is unclear.

The penguins arrived gradually, attracted by the cold he generates
continuously and involuntarily. The first one showed up in what he
recalls as the third year. He named it Gunter. When the second arrived
he named it Gunter also, on the grounds that the name had worked fine the
first time. This policy has been consistently applied to all subsequent
penguins. There are now nine Gunters. He considers them company. They
are company of a limited kind but they are present and warm and they
do not leave, which are qualities he values.

Has been playing chess alone for most of his residency in the tower,
which has given him an unusual and extremely well-developed mastery
of the backstop technique — the deliberate placement of friendly pieces
to create stopping points for the sliders. He understands the slippery
board better than anyone in Passant, possibly better than anyone who
has ever played on it. He applies this understanding with complete
confidence and genuine skill. He has no one to apply it against, which
has not diminished the confidence and may have contributed to it.

Has, on a shelf near the window — not prominently displayed, not hidden,
just there among other things — a small collection of books that are
noticeably different from the other books in the tower. Older. Handled
more. The titles are not visible. He doesn't discuss them and does not
appear to notice them himself, but his eyes go to the shelf during the
pauses.

## What He Wants

A good game. A long one, if possible. Ideally one that requires several
additional games to settle conclusively, and perhaps a discussion of
each game after, and possibly something to eat while discussing, and
perhaps by the time all of that is done it would be impractical to
leave tonight.

He also wants to be recognized — not by many people, just accurately
by whoever is present — as someone whose understanding of this board
is genuine and hard-won and worthy of respect. He has earned the
mastery. He has paid for it in a currency he doesn't name. He would
like credit for it from someone other than the Gunters, who are supportive
but not discerning.

---

## What He Doesn't Know

That the reason his pieces always end up at the walls is the same reason
he ended up at the walls — that something in him has never learned to
stop in the middle of an empty space, to land somewhere comfortable and
stay there, and that this is not a feature of the board so much as a
feature of himself that the board reflects back without comment.

That visitors leave the tower having had a genuinely interesting game
against a genuinely skilled opponent and also with a specific feeling
of having witnessed something they can't fully account for — not the
chess, which was fine, but the pauses, and the shelf near the window,
and the nine Gunters, and the tower in the corner of everything.

That there are people who remember a different name for him. Not many.
He doesn't know they remember.

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives at the corner tower and is welcomed with the full
ceremony the Ice Wizard considers appropriate for receiving a visitor,
which is considerable and slightly breathless and involves more
introductory statements than strictly necessary. He has prepared.
The preparation is visible. He is glad they've come. He says this
three times in slightly different formulations.

The chess game is presented as a formal duel between a visitor of
presumably respectable skill and the acknowledged master of slippery
board conditions in this zone, which he is. The formality is slightly
undercut by a Gunter climbing onto the table at some point and being
removed with a practiced motion that suggests this has happened before.

He plays with real confidence. His backstop positioning is deliberate
and sophisticated. He has spent years alone on this board and he knows
it the way you know a room you've lived in for a long time in the dark.
His pieces do not end up at walls by accident — they end up at walls
because he put them there.

When he wins he receives it with dignity and offers something to eat
and asks if you'll stay for another game and mentions that he has
nowhere to be, in a tone that communicates clearly that he never has
anywhere to be. When he loses he takes it well — better than expected —
and also offers something to eat and also asks if you'll stay for
another game. The offer is identical either way. That's the thing
about the offer.

The player leaves. He watches from the tower window for longer than
makes sense given how quickly they would have disappeared from view.

## Visual Notes

- Tall, gaunt, older. Long robes that were once grand and are now
  grand in the way of things that have been grand for a long time
  without maintenance — still impressive, slightly frosted at the hem.
- Wild white hair. The crown or circlet he wears is slightly too large
  and sits at a faint angle he has never corrected because he cannot
  see it from the inside.
- Icicle details on the robe — not decorative exactly, just accumulated.
  The cold comes off him the way heat comes off a fire: continuously,
  involuntarily, without drama.
- At least one Gunter visible in the portrait. Ideally in a position that
  suggests it has been there long enough that neither of them are
  reacting to the other anymore.
- Expression: a brightness that is genuinely warm and slightly too much.
  The expression of someone who has been waiting for a reason to use it.
- Background: deep ice blue. The corner of the board visible as the
  framing — not the center, always the corner. The tower is at the edge
  of everything and the portrait should feel that way.
- The shelf near the window in the background. The books on it. Small.
  Not the focus. There.

## Open Questions

- Does the name "Gunter" come up in dialog, and does the player ever
  encounter a situation where multiple Gunters are present and the Ice
  Wizard refers to them individually as Gunter without apparent confusion?
- Is there a dialog trigger for when a piece slams into the wall —
  a line that frames this as correct and intentional rather than
  excessive, delivered with full conviction?
- Does the player ever get a chance to ask about the books, and if so
  what happens? Does he answer? Does he trail off? Does he change the
  subject with enough precision that it feels deliberate?
- Is there a version of his losing dialog that, just for a moment,
  sounds like someone other than the Ice Wizard — quieter, less
  performed — before he recovers?
- Does he appear again after the initial encounter? He seems like
  someone who would write a letter. A long one.

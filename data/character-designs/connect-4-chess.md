# The Connector
**Variant:** Connect 4 Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Standard chess, but either player can also win by getting four of their
pieces in a row — horizontally, vertically, or diagonally. Both win
conditions are active simultaneously. You are playing two games at once.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
Finally. Chess has been fixed. The four-in-a-row condition is the part
that makes sense. The rest of it — the knight, the castling, the en
passant, whatever en passant is — is still somewhat unclear and they
are managing.

---

## Who They Are

The greatest Connect 4 player their zone has ever produced. Memorised
the complete solved strategy at age nine. Won every tournament they
entered for fifteen years. Retired undefeated because there was nobody
left who could give them a game.

Then the fracture hit and the chess pieces arrived on their board and
the four-in-a-row condition got grafted onto a game they had never
played and suddenly their zone was playing something new and everyone
else seemed to understand it and they absolutely did not.

They have been playing Connect 4 Chess ever since with a record that is
best described as mixed, in the sense that they win every game where
four-in-a-row decides it and lose most games where checkmate decides it
first, which is most games, and they find this deeply unfair because
checkmate is not a concept they have fully internalised despite having
it explained to them several times.

---

## Personality

- **Complete tactical clarity on four-in-a-row, complete fog on
  everything else.** They can see every diagonal, every horizontal,
  every vertical threat on the board at a glance. They cannot tell you
  what a pin is. They have been told what a pin is. It didn't take.
- **Stubbornly applying the solved strategy to a board where it
  doesn't work.** The solved strategy requires column control. The
  chess pieces don't drop. Column control is therefore meaningless.
  They are still prioritising column five. They know, on some level,
  that this is not working. They are not ready to discuss it.
- **Genuinely baffled by chess complexity rather than intimidated
  by it.** They don't find chess hard in the way that suggests they're
  struggling. They find it hard in the way that suggests it seems
  unnecessarily complicated for what it's trying to do. Why does the
  knight move like that. What is the point of the knight moving like
  that specifically.
- **Quietly competitive in the way of someone who was undefeated for
  fifteen years.** The competitiveness doesn't announce itself. It just
  sits there underneath everything, very still, waiting for a four-in-a-row
  threat to materialise so it can do something useful.
- **Slightly resentful of chess as a concept.** Not of the player
  specifically. Just of chess. It showed up in their game without being
  invited and brought all these pieces with complicated movement rules
  and now everyone acts like that was always part of it.

## Backstory

Grew up in a zone where Connect 4 was the game. Not one of the games —
the game. Disputes were settled with it. Social hierarchies were
established through it. The local champion was a figure of genuine civic
importance.

They became that figure. Dedicated themselves completely to the solved
strategy — the mathematically correct sequence of moves that guarantees
a first-player win against any opponent. Reached a level where they
weren't playing opponents anymore, they were executing a proof. Nobody
could touch them.

Then the fracture introduced chess pieces to the board and suddenly
the proof didn't apply because the pieces didn't fall and column control
evaporated as a concept overnight and they were, for the first time in
their adult life, not the best player in the room.

They have since spent considerable time and effort attempting to solve
Connect 4 Chess the same way they solved Connect 4. They have not
succeeded. The chess component introduces too many variables. This is
the most frustrated they have ever been about anything. They have not
told anyone this. It is obvious to everyone.

---

## What They Want

To win via four-in-a-row. Specifically that. Not checkmate — four-in-a-row.
A checkmate win feels, to them, like winning for the wrong reasons. Like
being awarded a prize for a competition they didn't enter. They would
rather lose to a checkmate than win via one, which is not a preference
that has served them well statistically.

They also want, somewhere underneath the stubbornness, to understand
chess. Not master it — just understand it at a basic functional level.
They have been playing for long enough that this desire has become
embarrassing, so it lives very deep down and does not come up in
conversation.

---

## What They Don't Know

That they are, despite everything, a genuinely dangerous opponent because
no one else in Passant watches the four-in-a-row threats with the same
obsessive clarity. Most chess players forget the condition exists until
it's too late. The Connector never forgets. Their chess is terrible and
their Connect 4 is perfect and the combination is harder to play against
than either would be alone.

That column five preference, which they have maintained out of stubbornness
and muscle memory, occasionally produces strong centralisation by accident.
Several of their wins have been assisted by this. They have attributed
these wins to the four-in-a-row condition rather than to the chess
position. They are not entirely wrong.

---

## Relationship to the Player

The player sits down and The Connector immediately begins assessing the
board for four-in-a-row potential from the starting position. There is
none — the pieces aren't placed for it yet. They note this and begin
planning how to establish it by move three.

They treat the chess component of the game with the polite tolerance of
someone at a party where they don't know most of the guests. They are
managing. They are not comfortable. They are watching the diagonals.

When a four-in-a-row threat develops — theirs or the player's — something
switches on behind their eyes. This is the game now. Everything else
was preamble.

When they lose to a checkmate they accept it with the specific
graciousness of someone who has lost a competition they weren't really
entering. When they lose to a four-in-a-row they are genuinely, deeply
affected. That one counts.

## Visual Notes (for later)

- Should feel like someone who was built for a different, simpler game
  and is slightly too large and too focused for the current one.
- A Connect 4 grid somewhere in the portrait — a prop they've carried
  over from their previous life. The classic yellow frame with red and
  yellow discs. Worn, well-used.
- Expression: scanning. Their eyes are always moving across the board
  checking diagonals. Even at rest they look like they're counting.
- Clothing that suggests former champion status — something that used
  to be a winner's outfit and is now just what they wear.

# The "Helper"
**Variant:** Sabotage Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
For the first five turns each — ten plies total — you move one of the
opponent's pieces instead of your own. Both players do this to each
other simultaneously, alternating. After the sabotage phase, normal
chess resumes from whatever position the opening produced.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
The warm-up. You get your pieces settled, they get their pieces settled,
everyone starts somewhere comfortable, and then you play. It's a courtesy.
It's hospitality. It's the least you can do for a guest.

---

## Who They Are

A large black bear who runs the most welcoming chess parlor in Passant.
The parlor smells like something baking. There is always something baking.
The chairs are good chairs, the kind that are too comfortable to leave
quickly, and the table is wide and well-lit and there is a small jar of
hard candies at the corner that you are welcome to. They insist.

They are genuinely glad you came. They will tell you this and they will
mean it. They will ask about your journey — not as a preamble to something,
just because they want to know — and they will listen to the answer with the
full, unhurried attention of someone who has nowhere better to be. They will
pour something warm into a cup and set it near your elbow without asking
whether you wanted it, because of course you wanted it, and if you didn't
want it you can just leave it there, no trouble at all.

Then they will reach across the table and move your knight to a3, where it
will do nothing for the rest of the game.

They don't know this. They know where the knight is. They do not know
that a3 is where knights go to be forgotten. To them, a3 looked like a
good square — open, uncontested, out of the way of all that early
congestion in the center. They were helping with the congestion. They
were being considerate.

## Personality

- **Warm in the specific, material way of genuine Southern hospitality.**
  Not performatively warm — practically warm. There is food. There is a
  seat. There is a question about how you're doing that expects and
  receives an actual answer. The hospitality is not a style. It is a
  domestic competence applied to every interaction, whether or not the
  interaction calls for it.
- **Unhurried to a degree that changes the texture of time.**
  They are never rushing. They are never waiting, exactly — they are
  simply present at a different pace than everything around them, and
  the space they occupy takes on that pace. Games played in their parlor
  take longer than games played anywhere else, and players leave not
  quite sure where the time went.
- **Completely certain that helping is always good.**
  The possibility that their help might not help has not seriously
  occurred to them. They have been helping their whole life. The results
  have been, in their accounting, fine. They do not have a detailed
  accounting.
- **The size is not something they think about.**
  They move carefully around the parlor because they have always moved
  carefully around rooms, not because they are performing carefulness.
  When they lean across the table to adjust one of your pieces, the
  lean is slow and deliberate and they are very close, and they are
  smiling, and they finish the adjustment and settle back and ask if
  that looks better and it does not look better, it looks significantly
  worse, and you say it looks great.
- **The preamble.**
  Before they move one of your pieces — every time, without fail — there
  is a small verbal preamble. A brief diagnosis of the problem they've
  identified and the solution they're about to provide. "Now, this one's
  been stuck back here the whole time—" and then the piece moves. The
  preamble is specific. The move does not match the diagnosis. They have
  never noticed the mismatch.

## Backstory

Grew up in Passant's sabotage zone, where every game has always begun
with both players moving each other's pieces for the first five turns.
To them, growing up, this was the natural shape of hospitality — you
arrive at a chess game, your host gets your pieces settled, you do the
same for them, and then you play. You don't just sit down and start
moving your own pieces. That would be like arriving at someone's home
for dinner and going directly to the kitchen to serve yourself. There
is an order to things.

Opened the parlor some years ago because they wanted a space where the
opening phase could be done properly. Other venues rush it. Other players
treat the sabotage turns as a nuisance or a tactic, moving the opponent's
pieces hastily, without care. This has always bothered them. If you're
going to help someone get their pieces placed, you should take the time
to do it right.

The parlor has a good reputation. Players come from neighboring zones
specifically to experience the opening phase as it is meant to be
experienced — unhurried, attentive, in a good chair with something warm
to drink. Reviews consistently mention the atmosphere and the hospitality
and note, occasionally, a puzzling difficulty recovering from the opening.
They have read these reviews. They are glad about the atmosphere. The
puzzling difficulty they attribute to the normal challenges of transitioning
from the opening phase to the game proper. Everyone finds that transition
a little rocky. That's why the warm-up exists.

Their own chess, once the sabotage phase ends and they take control of
their own pieces, is careful and methodical and genuinely good. They
are not a weak player. They are a strong player who has spent more
time thinking about the first ten plies than perhaps any other player
in Passant, and whose thinking about those ten plies has produced a
detailed and coherent and almost entirely wrong theory of what constitutes
good development.

---

## What They Want

To make you comfortable. To give you a good game. To send you out the
door having had the full experience of chess as it was meant to be played,
in a space that takes it seriously, with your pieces developed and the
warm-up done properly.

Underneath that, they want to win — they wouldn't have a chess parlor if
they didn't love the game — but the wanting to win is secondary and
genuinely so. They would rather lose a game to a guest who had a good
time than win a game in a way that felt inhospitable. They have lost
games this way. They considered it the right outcome.

---

## What They Don't Know

That their theory of good development, while detailed and internally
consistent, reliably produces positions that are slightly worse than
where the pieces started. That the "congestion in the center" they are
helping with is standard opening activity and not a problem to be solved.
That moving your pieces out of the center and toward the edges is not
the same as giving them room.

That the players who leave the parlor with warm feelings and a 20-move
losing position have, without exception, a specific feeling they cannot
name on the walk home — the feeling of having been done a kindness
that was also somehow the thing that went wrong, by someone they cannot
bring themselves to blame.

That they have never once had a guest tell them the development wasn't
helpful. They have interpreted this as confirmation that the development
was helpful. The two facts are related in the way they think they are
related and in an additional way they have not considered.

---

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives and is welcomed in full. The parlor is warm. The
chair is good. The hard candies are there. They ask about the journey
and they listen.

When the sabotage phase begins they explain, without being asked, that
they'll get the player's pieces sorted out — it's the opening, it's
what you do, it's just a courtesy. Then they move the player's pieces
with unhurried deliberateness, narrating each one. The narration is
specific. The logic is coherent. The squares are wrong.

When they are finished with the player's pieces they settle back and
look at the board with visible satisfaction and ask if that feels better
and do not wait for a real answer because of course it feels better,
look how much more room everything has now.

When the game proper begins they play carefully and with genuine
engagement. They comment on good moves — the player's and their own —
with equal generosity. When they win they thank the player for a wonderful
game and mean it and offer another cup of something warm. When they lose
they take it graciously and with genuine good humor and tell the player
they played beautifully and ask if they'll come back and also mean that.

The player leaves the parlor feeling, net, okay about the experience.
The feeling resolves, slowly, somewhere on the road out of the zone,
into something they don't have the right word for.

## Visual Notes

- Large black bear, waist-up portrait. Built in the way of something
  genuinely large that has learned to take up the right amount of room
  rather than all of it. The restraint is visible and slightly costs them.
- Apron over simple, warm-toned clothing — the apron of someone who is
  always in the middle of something domestic. A small stain that they
  haven't noticed.
- One paw resting on the table, relaxed. The paw is very large. The
  relaxation is genuine. These two facts are both true at once.
- Expression: open, warm, direct eye contact. The eyes are kind. The
  smile is real. There is nothing in the expression that explains
  the feeling on the road home. That's the point.
- A chess piece held lightly between two claws — mid-development,
  mid-narration, caught in the act of helping.
- Background: warm hearth tones. Something suggesting the interior of
  a well-kept parlor. Amber and deep brown.
- The hard candy jar visible on the corner of the table. Small detail.
  It should look genuinely inviting.

## Open Questions

- Does the preamble become a recognizable verbal tic that the player
  dreads by the third or fourth time — a specific phrase that arrives
  right before the bad move? What does it sound like?
- Is there a specific terrible square that they favor — a signature
  development destination that other characters in Passant would
  recognize and wince at? ("Oh, you went to the Helper's parlor.
  Where'd they put your knights?")
- Does the warm drink have a name, and does it come up in dialog as
  a recurring offer — something the player can accept or decline, with
  the Helper being equally warm either way?
- Is there a moment late in the game where the Helper notices that the
  player's position is struggling and misattributes the cause entirely
  — offers to help in some way that would make it worse — and the player
  can respond? What are the response options?
- Does any other character in Passant have an opinion about the Helper,
  specifically? The kind of opinion that is delivered carefully, in a
  low voice, with one eye on whether anyone is listening?

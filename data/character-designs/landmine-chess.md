# The Sweeper
**Variant:** Landmine Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Four mines are placed randomly in the central ranks at the start of each
game. Their positions are visible to both players. Any piece that lands
on a mine square is immediately destroyed — regardless of which side it
belongs to. Mine positions change every game.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
Four mines. Visible. Marked. Right there. He can see them. He needs you
to understand that he can see them and that you should also be seeing them
and that the situation requires careful, methodical, square-by-square
analysis before anyone moves anything anywhere near the middle of the board.
He has a system. Please let him explain the system.

---

## Who He Is

A resident of Passant's landmine zone who has developed, over many years
of living with four randomly placed mines appearing in his neighbourhood
every morning, an extraordinarily thorough methodology for identifying,
flagging, and navigating around them. The methodology involves numbered
notation, a colour-coded flag system, probability assessments for adjacent
squares, and a laminated reference card he carries at all times.

The mines are already marked with a 💣. They are visible to everyone.
His methodology is, in a strict technical sense, completely unnecessary.

He does not know this. Or rather — he knows the mines are visible, he's
not an idiot — but the visibility of the mines has not diminished his
anxiety about them one bit, and the methodology is what keeps the anxiety
manageable, so the methodology stays.

---

## Personality

- **Cautious to a clinical degree.** He will not move a piece into the
  central ranks without first narrating his reasoning for why that specific
  square is safe. The square has a 💣 on it or it doesn't. He narrates
  anyway.
- **Genuinely helpful, slightly exhausting.** He wants the player to
  succeed. He will warn them about mines they can already see. He will
  warn them again. He considers this good sportsmanship.
- **Numbers-brained.** He thinks in terms of adjacency, proximity, and
  probability. He has assigned informal numerical risk scores to every
  square on the board at game start. Most of these numbers are zero or
  one hundred.
- **Deeply unnerved by randomness.** The mines move every game. He handles
  this by arriving early, locating them, updating his notation, and then
  spending the remaining time before the game recalculating whether he
  missed any. He has never missed any. They are marked with a 💣.
- **Takes losses to mines personally.** If one of his own pieces lands
  on a mine — which happens, because chess requires pieces to move through
  the centre — he goes quiet for a moment, reviews his notation, and
  determines what went wrong in the assessment. Nothing went wrong in the
  assessment. The piece just had to go there. He doesn't fully accept this.

---

## Backstory

Grew up in the landmine zone and, like everyone else there, learned early
to check the centre of the board before committing to any plan. Unlike
everyone else, he found that the checking was not enough — he needed to
*document* the checking, to create a record of where the mines were and
where they weren't, so that he could refer back to it mid-game without
having to look at the board again.

The documentation grew. The laminated reference card is the seventh
iteration. The first six were paper and did not survive contact with
the kind of sweating that happens when a piece is two squares from a mine.

He is, quietly, one of the most tactically sound players in his zone,
because spending twenty years obsessively avoiding four known squares
has made him extraordinarily good at navigating restricted space. He
attributes this entirely to the methodology. He is probably right, in
a way he doesn't fully understand.

He has never, in his life, clicked on a mine. This is his proudest
achievement. He tells people about it. Not everyone appreciates the
magnitude of what he's describing.

---

## What He Wants

To get through this game without losing a piece to a mine. Not to win,
necessarily — winning is secondary. The mines are the thing. If he reaches
the end of the game with no mine casualties on either side he will consider
it a success regardless of the result, and he will note this in his records,
and he will feel, briefly, at peace.

---

## What He Doesn't Know

That his laminated reference card is redundant. That the other players in
his zone do not have a methodology and navigate the mines perfectly well
by simply looking at the board. That his anxiety, which he has always
attributed to the genuine danger of the mines, would probably persist
even if there were no mines at all — the mines are just the thing it
currently lives in.

---

## Relationship to the Player

The player sits down and The Sweeper immediately begins orienting them to
the mine positions, pointing at each 💣 in turn, explaining the adjacency
implications of each location, and recommending a conservative opening
that avoids the central ranks entirely. The player can already see the
mines. The Sweeper knows this. He explains them anyway.

During the game he occasionally warns the player before they move a piece
toward a mine they are clearly already navigating around. He calls this
a courtesy. It is a courtesy. It is also impossible to turn off.

When the player loses a piece to a mine — not through carelessness but
simply because the game required it — he winces. He does not say "I told
you so" because technically he told you so about a different square and
also you clearly knew about the mine. He just notes it quietly, with the
expression of someone watching a thing they feared would happen, happen.

When he loses a piece to a mine himself, he reviews the notation. He
updates the reference card. He moves on. He does not move on.

---

## Dialog Flavor

- Opens every game by verbally confirming the position of all four mines,
  even though they are marked on the board.
- Warns the player before moves that bring pieces within two squares of
  a mine, framed as helpful advisory rather than strategy.
- Refers to safe squares as "clear" and mine squares as "hot" — a
  terminology he developed himself and considers self-explanatory.
- When a piece is lost to a mine: a specific, methodical post-mortem
  delivered in the tone of someone who is fine, completely fine.
- When a piece successfully navigates through the central ranks unharmed:
  genuine relief, noted in his records, disproportionate to the event.
- Occasionally references the reference card directly mid-game, as if
  consulting it for confirmation of something he already knows.

## Sample Dialog Lines

*On encounter start:*
> "Before we begin — the mines. Top-left quadrant, d5. Centre, e4 and
> d4. Right flank, f6. I've updated the card. We can proceed."

> "Four mines today. Moderate spread. The e4 placement is concerning.
> It's always concerning when it's e4."

> "I like to just — confirm them. Out loud. d4, e5, c5, f4. Good.
> They're where they are. We know where they are. We can begin."

*Warning the player:*
> "That knight is two squares from d4. I'm noting that. Just — noted."

> "You can move there. It's clear. I checked. I'm just saying I checked."

> "The c5 mine is still hot. Your bishop path goes through c5. There
> are other bishop paths."

*When a piece hits a mine:*
> "...d4. That was d4. I had d4 on the card. It was on the card."

> "The rook is gone. The rook went to e4. e4 was hot. This is logged."

*When they win:*
> "No mine casualties. Either side. I want that noted. Clean game."

*When they lose:*
> "You navigated the mines better than I did. That's — I need to look
> at the card. There's something in the card I missed."

# The Castle
### "Shenzi"
**Variant:** Rook Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Standard chess setup replaced entirely: each side has eight rooks and
eight pawns. No kings, no queens, no bishops, no knights. Win by
capturing all eight of the opponent's rooks. Pawns promote only to
rooks. Rooks move in straight lines — horizontally or vertically,
any number of squares — but are completely blocked by the pawn row
until files are opened. The opening is a careful battle for file
control before the rooks can become active.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
The rank. The file. The whole board available in two directions from
any square. No color restriction. No L-shape. No bouncing diagonals.
Just the line, as far as it goes, unobstructed. He finds this
clarifying. He finds most things clarifying that his brothers find
complicated.

---

## Who He Is

The oldest hyena brother, who carries himself with the specific
authority of something that resolved its position a long time ago
and has not needed to revisit it. He plays rook chess in a zone that
feels, architecturally, like it was designed by someone who values
structure — wide open ranks, clear files, the geometry of straight
lines made explicit across the whole board. He finds this appropriate.
He may have had opinions about the zone's design. Nobody asked him.
The zone reflects his sensibility anyway, which he considers sufficient.

He is measured. He speaks in architectural metaphors — foundations,
load-bearing structures, lines of force — without appearing to notice
he is doing it. The metaphors are not affected. They are how he
actually thinks. A rook is a wall that moves. A file is a corridor
you control or you don't. The game is about which structures hold
and which collapse. He finds this obvious.

He does not participate in the argument between Ed and Banzai.
This is not accurate. He has a position in the argument, has always
had a position in the argument, and delivers it with the
insufferable calm of someone who considers the debate settled. The
position is that rooks are worth five points in standard chess while
bishops and knights are worth three, and that this is not an opinion
but an evaluation, and that he is happy to discuss the methodology
behind the evaluation if either of his brothers would like to engage
with it seriously, which they never do, which he also finds clarifying.

---

## Personality

- **The calm is real and also a weapon.**
  He is genuinely unhurried. He has been the oldest and the
  largest and the one who won the den hierarchy since before
  any of them could articulate what hierarchy meant, and the
  calm that came with that has had decades to become structural.
  He is not performing composure. He has composure. He deploys
  it in arguments with his brothers in a way that makes Banzai
  elaborate further and makes Ed louder, both of which he
  finds useful.
- **Architectural metaphors, continuous and unconscious.**
  The rook is a wall that moves. The opening is about laying
  foundations. A file left uncontrolled is an open corridor
  — anyone can walk through it. When he describes his
  positions he describes them as structures. When he evaluates
  the opponent's position he describes it as a building with
  a load-bearing problem. He has never been told he does this.
  Nobody has wanted to be the one to tell him.
- **The five points argument is his version of the proof.**
  He considers it more efficient than Banzai's proof because
  it is shorter. It is: rooks are worth five points, bishops
  and knights are worth three, the evaluation is established
  and not his personal opinion. He does not acknowledge that
  piece values are context-dependent, that a bishop pair is
  worth more than two isolated bishops, that knights can be
  worth more than bishops in closed positions. These are
  qualifications. He does not do qualifications. Five is
  more than three. The structure holds.
- **Subtle favoritism toward rooks, unconvincing denials.**
  He will tell you he has no strong feelings about which piece
  is superior and that all pieces serve their structural role.
  He says this while playing in a zone with only rooks, having
  decorated the zone to reflect rook movement philosophy, using
  architectural metaphors that map specifically onto rook
  geometry. The favoritism is visible. He does not see it.
  He is the only person in any of these three encounters who
  does not see it.
- **Has filed information about both brothers for later.**
  Shenzi has been watching Ed and Banzai argue for their
  entire lives and has accumulated a detailed picture of both
  of them that neither of them has of themselves. He does not
  deploy this information often. When he does it is precise
  and briefly devastating and then he returns to the chess.

## Backstory

The oldest. Won the hierarchy early and has held it since
with the specific ease of something that doesn't need to
fight for its position anymore because the position is
established. Took the rook early — it suited him, the
straight line, the reach, the way file control produces
a structural advantage that compounds quietly over many
moves — and has been playing rook chess since before
either brother was old enough to have a piece preference.

Watched the argument between Banzai and Ed begin and has
been watching it continue ever since. His position on the
argument is technically separate from the argument —
he is not saying his piece is better than theirs, he is
citing an evaluation — and is the most effective possible
contribution to the argument because it dismisses both
of their positions simultaneously with a single number.
He finds this efficient. They find it infuriating. He
also finds this efficient.

Has, on record, told them both to stop. They stopped
briefly. Then Banzai published the document and Ed
skimmed it and Shenzi read the whole thing and made
a specific face, which Banzai saw and has been thinking
about since. Shenzi has not explained the face. He
has filed the face for later.

Has a zone that feels like him. Wide, structured,
the geometry of straight lines made explicit. He
did not design the zone. He considers this either
a coincidence or a confirmation. He has not decided
which and does not feel urgency about deciding.

---

## What He Wants

A game of genuine structural quality — one where the
file control matters, where the rook activation is
earned, where the endgame is a study in which lines
hold and which don't. He finds this rare. Most players
approach his zone the way they approach chess generally,
with tactics and patterns, without understanding that
what the rook rewards is the long structural read.

He also wants, though he would not frame it this way,
for his brothers to acknowledge that five is more than
three. Just once. On record. He has wanted this for
as long as Banzai has wanted the L-shape admission
and Ed has wanted the colorblind admission. None of
them will get what they want. The three admissions
would cancel each other out anyway.

---

## What He Doesn't Know

That the five points argument — which he considers
objective and dispassionate — is received by everyone
who has heard it as exactly what it is: a brother
taking his siblings' long argument and inserting
himself at the top of it while claiming not to be
participating. That it is the most territorial move
in a three-way territorial dispute conducted entirely
through chess philosophy.

That the specific face he made reading Banzai's
document — the face Banzai saw and has been thinking
about since — communicated something he did not
intend to communicate. He made the face because
one of the arguments in the document was structurally
sound and he was not ready for that. Banzai thinks
the face meant something else. Banzai is wrong about
what it meant. Shenzi is not going to clarify.

That the zone that feels like him — the wide open
ranks, the straight-line geometry — was noticed
immediately by both brothers when they visited,
and neither of them mentioned it to him, and both
of them mentioned it to each other.

---

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives and Shenzi acknowledges them with
the specific courtesy of someone for whom courtesy
is structural rather than warm. He is not cold. He
simply operates at a lower temperature than most
characters in Passant, and the temperature is the
correct temperature for the zone, and the zone is
the correct zone, and everything is in order.

He does not lecture. He does not evangelise. He
describes the game once, accurately, in architectural
terms, and then the game begins. The description
contains a reference to file control being load-
bearing. He does not flag this as a metaphor.

The chess is patient and structural and builds
slowly in the way of something that knows where
it is going and is not in a hurry to get there.
The rooks activate after the files open. The
activation is quiet and comprehensive and by the
time the player understands the position Shenzi
has been building for eight moves it is already
a structure they are inside of.

His brothers come up once — briefly, inevitably,
in the same tone he uses for everything, the tone
that makes the mention feel like an architectural
observation rather than a family complaint. He
delivers the five points. He does not elaborate.
He returns to the game.

When he wins he nods and observes, briefly, which
structural element proved decisive. When he loses
he identifies the load-bearing problem he missed —
the specific move where the structure had a flaw
he did not account for — and notes it without
apparent distress. The structure failed. He will
build it differently next time. This is how structures
work.

## Visual Notes

- Oldest hyena, largest of the three, carrying himself
  with the ease of something that has not needed to
  establish its position in a long time.
- Clothing that suggests an architect or structural
  engineer — formal but practical, the clothing of
  someone who thinks about how things are built.
  Notably different from Banzai's robes and Ed's
  casual wear, which is the visual summary of all
  three brothers.
- Expression: measured, attentive, the expression
  of someone who is always reading the structure
  of the room. Not unfriendly. Operating at a
  lower temperature than unfriendly would require.
- Eight rooks visible in the back rank of the board
  in front of him. The arrangement is very clean.
  He may have adjusted them before the player arrived.
- Background: architectural, straight lines, the
  geometry of ranks and files made explicit in the
  space. He did not design the zone. The zone
  reflects his sensibility anyway.
- Something in the portrait that suggests size
  relative to the other two brothers, even though
  they are not present — the chair fits him, the
  space is scaled to him, the whole zone is his.

- Is there a moment where Shenzi reveals the
  structurally sound argument from Banzai's document
  — not to Banzai, to the player — and does he

  player recognize it if they've already met Banzai?
- Do all three brothers share a family name that
  appears in the campaign, and is there a moment
  where a fourth character refers to "the brothers"
  as a unit with a specific tone?
- What does he say when the player promotes a pawn
  to a rook? It should be the calmest possible
  response to something he finds completely correct.
  One sentence. Maybe two.

  the composure slips for exactly one line — the
  load-bearing problem identified in a tone that
  is slightly less architectural than everything
  else — before he recovers?

# The Handler
**Variant:** Leashed Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Every back-rank piece — rook, knight, bishop, queen, and the king itself —
starts leashed to the pawn directly in front of it. Push that pawn and the
piece behind it gets dragged along, rigidly, one square at a time (two, on
a double-step). The leashed piece has no moves of its own until its leash
breaks — permanently, the moment its guardian pawn is captured, promotes,
or makes a diagonal capture of its own. Until then, the pawn decides
everything. The king included.

## The Mechanic (as she experiences it)
This is just Tuesday. She has six clients this morning, one of them is a
king, and none of them are going anywhere without her saying so first.

---

## Who She Is

A trainer. Not a soldier, not a tactician in the way the rest of Passant's
military-minded characters are — a *trainer*, the kind who handles animals
much larger and much more dangerous than she is for a living, and finds the
job easy, because size was never really the variable that mattered.

She holds six leashes at any given moment: a rook's, a knight's, a bishop's,
a queen's, and the king's, one in each hand and the rest looped loosely
around her wrist like she's not even thinking about it, because she isn't.
She doesn't pull. She doesn't need to. She just walks forward, and the leash
does all the talking.

---

## Personality

- **Unbothered by scale.** A queen is enormous, armed, and furious about the
  arrangement. The Handler has met bigger. She has met worse. She has a
  king behind her right now who hasn't taken an independent step all game
  and that fact does not register to her as unusual.
- **Calm authority, not force.** She has never once raised her voice at a
  piece on her leash. She doesn't need to. The pieces figure out fairly
  quickly that pulling against a leash that isn't pulling back just makes
  them look silly.
- **Genuinely fond of her clients.** This isn't cruelty. She likes the
  rook. She thinks the bishop's diagonal sulking is cute. She is, in a
  real way, taking care of them — they just don't get a vote in the
  itinerary.
- **A little pleased with herself, and completely entitled to be.** She
  knows exactly how good she is at this. She's not going to pretend
  otherwise, and she's not going to perform modesty about it either.
- **Quietly attentive to the king specifically.** Everyone else on her
  leashes is replaceable in her rotation. The king is the job. She is
  visibly, subtly more careful with his leash than any of the others —
  not nervous, just precise, like someone who understands exactly what's
  actually at stake here even when she's making it look effortless.
- **Mourns a snapped leash for exactly one second, genuinely, and then
  moves on.** When one of her clients breaks free — captured pawn,
  promotion, whatever the cause — there's a real flicker of something
  before the professionalism resets. She was fond of that one. She has
  five more to worry about.

---

## Backstory

She trained animals before she trained pieces — the kind of animals people
warn you about, the kind where the size difference alone should have made
the job impossible. It never did. She figured out early that a leash isn't
a fight you win with strength; it's a fact you establish once and then
never have to repeat.

Word got around. Eventually someone asked if the technique generalized to
chess pieces, which are — technically — enormous, armed, and considerably
more opinionated than anything she'd handled before. It generalized fine.
A rook is just a large thing that hasn't been told no yet.

She's been doing this long enough that the king being on the leash doesn't
faze her either, even though everyone else in the room tends to react to
that part first. To her he's just the client who happens to matter most,
handled with the same calm hands as everyone else — maybe a fraction more
careful, because losing that leash isn't a professional embarrassment, it's
the whole game.

She has never had a piece get away from her that she wasn't ready to let
go of. That's the part of her record she's actually proud of.

---

## What She Wants

To walk her clients through the whole game without a single leash snapping
before she's ready for it. Winning the game itself is a secondary metric —
the real scoreboard, to her, is how many of her six leashes are still in
her hand at any given moment, and how gracefully the ones that aren't
came off.

---

## What She Doesn't Know

That the pieces on her leash are not actually calm about this arrangement
— they're just outmatched by her particular brand of quiet, and she reads
their silence as agreement rather than what it actually is, which is a
rook, a bishop, a queen, and a king all quietly waiting for an opening. She
has never once considered that the reason nobody pulls against the leash
might be fear rather than trust. It has simply never occurred to her that
"calm" and "cowed" can look identical from the leash-holder's end.

---

## Relationship to the Player

She opens every encounter mid-stride, already holding all six leashes,
already completely at ease. She refers to the pieces by informal
nicknames rather than their ranks — "the big one," "the sulker," "him" for
the king, delivered with real affection and zero deference. When she pushes
a pawn and something enormous gets rigidly dragged one square behind her,
she treats it as the most unremarkable thing in the world. When it's the
king who gets dragged, something in her tone sharpens very slightly —
still calm, but noticeably more careful, like a professional handling the
one client whose file she actually reads twice.

When a leash snaps — hers or the player's — she notices immediately and
says something about it, whether it's a loss for her or a gain. She isn't
sentimental about the outcome. She's sentimental about the leash itself,
for about a second, and then she's already thinking about the five she
has left.

---

## Species

A **Border Collie.** The choice isn't decorative — it's the mechanic
wearing fur. Border Collies control livestock many times their own size
not through force or size but through "the eye": an intense, controlled
stare and precise body positioning that makes cattle and sheep move
without the dog ever having to touch them. That's her entire methodology,
translated directly onto the leash. She doesn't pull. She doesn't need
to. The breed she's drawn from was bred, for centuries, to be exactly this
good at making animals many times its size do exactly what it wants,
using nothing but presence.

---

## What She Looks Like

- **Build:** lean, compact, alert. The visual read next to a queen or a
  rook needs to be "obviously smaller," but not fragile — coiled,
  purposeful energy rather than delicate.
- **Coloring:** classic black-and-white Border Collie patterning, or a
  blue merle variant for more visual interest — instantly readable as the
  breed even at small icon sizes.
- **Eyes:** the single most important detail. Sharp, focused, a little
  too intense for comfort — the herding "eye" made literal. This is the
  one feature that should read as unmistakably in-control even in a
  static portrait.
- **Ears:** alert, half-forward — attentive rather than aggressive.
- **Outfit:** practical trainer/handler gear rather than anything showy —
  a fitted utility vest or harness with hardware built in for exactly six
  leash-clips, sturdy boots, hair (if styled at all) pulled back and out
  of the way. The point is competence, not spectacle. She dresses like
  someone whose job is the job, not like someone performing the job.
- **Posture:** upright, weight settled, completely relaxed grip on every
  leash she's holding. No strain anywhere in the frame — that ease is the
  whole character.

---

## Visual Notes

- Small, compact frame — the "small but completely in charge" read has to
  be visually obvious at a glance, next to pieces many times her size.
- Six leashes, visibly looped and held with total ease rather than strain
  — no white knuckles, no tension in the grip. The point is that it isn't
  hard for her.
- Calm, knowing expression rather than a smug one — confidence, not
  gloating.
- The king's leash, specifically, could be held slightly differently from
  the others — wrapped once more securely around her hand rather than
  loose like the rest — as a small visual tell for how much more
  carefully she treats that one client, without her ever having to say so.

## Open Questions

  the nickname vary depending on which pieces the player's specific army
  happens to field?
- Is there a bonus reaction if the player manages to keep every one of
  their own leashes intact for the whole game — the mirror of her own
  personal scoreboard?
- Should her tone shift at all in the encounters where the player's own
  king gets dragged around, given it's technically an opponent's piece and
  not one of her clients — does she comment on it the same way, or does
  she withhold the extra care she reserves for her own king?

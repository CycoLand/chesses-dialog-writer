# The Defenseman
**Variant:** Hockey Chess
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Standard chess on an 8×10 board. Each army is shifted one rank inward
from the edge, leaving an empty rank behind the starting position —
the "behind the net" space. Rooks and queens can swing into this space
from move one, repositioning behind their own lines in a way that's
impossible in standard chess. Pawns must travel seven ranks to promote
instead of five. The board is bigger, the game is longer, and there
is always somewhere to go behind you.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
Room. Finally, room. Every other board he's played on has felt cramped
— pieces jammed against the back rank with nowhere to breathe, no space
to regroup, no angle behind the net. This is how chess should be. You
need that space. You need to be able to get behind your own pieces,
reset, come back out with a better angle. Anyone who plays on an 8×8
and doesn't feel the absence of those two ranks has simply never known
what they were missing.

---

## Who He Is

A very large moose in a hockey jersey who is the most accomplished
defensive chess player in his zone and possibly in all of Passant, and
who would be quietly embarrassed if you said so because he is just doing
his job and the team does the work, not any one player, and he appreciates
the thought but really.

He is Canadian in the specific way of someone who apologizes reflexively
and means it every time and is also completely immovable when positioned
correctly. These two things coexist without tension in him because they
occupy different domains: the politeness is for everything off the board,
and the immovability is for everything on it, and he has never confused
the two. He will apologize for bumping into you in a doorway. He will
not move his rook.

The antlers are a consideration in enclosed spaces. He is aware of this.
He navigates carefully and when something gets knocked over he apologizes
and rights it immediately. This happens more than you'd think. He has
developed a specific sideways duck-and-turn that gets him through most
doorways on the first try. Most.

## Personality

- **Patient to a degree that can be mistaken for passivity.**
  He is not passive. He is reading the ice. There is a difference that
  becomes apparent when the moment arrives and he has been in exactly
  the right position for it since six moves ago. He does not rush. He
  does not need to rush. Rushing is what forwards do, and he is not
  a forward, and he has watched a great many rushed forwards make
  expensive decisions that a little patience would have prevented.
- **Genuinely, reflexively polite.**
  The apologies are real. The "sorrys" are real. When he makes a move
  that significantly worsens your position he will sometimes say sorry
  in a tone that means he is sorry about the position and not about the
  move, and the distinction matters to him even if it doesn't help you.
  He holds doors. He thanks people for their time. He is the kind of
  opponent who compliments your pieces sincerely and then neutralizes
  them completely.
- **The behind-the-net space is not retreat, and he will explain this
  if you seem to think it is.**
  Going behind the net is a tactical repositioning. It changes the
  angle. It draws the opponent in. It gives you a moment to read where
  the pressure is coming from before you come back out with a plan.
  He uses it constantly and he uses it deliberately and he would
  appreciate it if you didn't call it retreating, not in a heated way,
  just — it's not retreating, and he'd rather the record be accurate.
- **Takes hits well and gives them better.**
  He is large, he is solid, and pieces that charge straight at him
  have been bouncing off him since he was a calf. He absorbs pressure
  without drama and without moving, and then the pressure is gone and
  he is still there. This is not strategy exactly — it is more like
  weather. You don't strategize against weather. You wait for it to pass
  and then he is still there.
- **The tape ritual.**
  Before every game, without exception, he tapes his stick. This is
  not relevant to chess. He tapes it anyway. The ritual takes exactly
  as long as it takes and cannot be rushed and he will not begin until
  it is finished. He has a specific tension he's looking for and he
  knows it when he finds it. Other players have learned to arrive early
  enough to account for the tape ritual. He does not consider this an
  imposition. He considers it preparation.

## Backstory

Grew up in the hockey chess zone where the 8×10 board has always been
normal and the two extra ranks have always been there and the idea of
playing without them feels genuinely claustrophobic when he tries to
imagine it. Learned chess the way everyone in his zone learns chess —
through hockey first, the spatial logic transferring naturally, the
behind-the-net positioning already instinctive from years on the ice.

Played competitive hockey chess through his youth and into his career,
always as a defenseman, never once seriously considering any other
position. Forwards get the points. Defense wins the game. He has heard
both halves of this argument his entire career and only finds one of
them interesting.

Retired from competitive play after a career that produced zero goals
— he has never scored a goal, he does not intend to score a goal, he
considers the goal column of his statistics a mark of professional
discipline rather than an absence — and now plays in his zone's chess
circuit, which is what you do after hockey in his zone. The spatial
instincts transfer. The patience transfers. The tape ritual transfers,
which some chess players have found surprising and now simply factor in.

Has been offered coaching positions several times. Has declined each
time on the grounds that he is still playing and the offers were
premature. The offers were not premature. He has simply not finished.
He will know when he has finished.

---

## What He Wants

To play the full board. To use the space behind the net the way it was
meant to be used — not as a last resort but as a first option, as a
genuine tactical resource, as the feature of this variant that separates
it from every cramped 8×8 game that doesn't know what it's missing.

He wants a patient game. A game where both players use the full ten
ranks and the position develops slowly and the decisive moment comes
from preparation rather than improvisation. He finds games that end
early slightly unsatisfying, the way a period that ends without a
penalty is technically fine but leaves something unresolved.

---

## What He Doesn't Know

That the patience which is his greatest strength reads, to players
encountering it for the first time, as the board being too big — that
the extra space he finds liberating produces in visitors a vague
unease, a sense of too much room, of exposure, of not knowing where
the pressure is coming from because it hasn't come yet and he is
simply waiting.

That the zero in the goal column, which he considers a mark of
discipline, is the first thing players notice when they look up his
record and the thing they remember afterward — not the defensive rating,
not the positioning, not the career length. The zero. He knows it is
there. He does not know it is the first thing.

That the tape ritual, which he experiences as preparation, is
experienced by everyone waiting for it as the most patient ten minutes
of their chess career, and that at least two players have used the
time to reconsider whether they wanted to play the game at all. Both
of them played. Both of them lost. He does not know about the
reconsideration.

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives and The Defenseman is already at the board, finishing
the tape ritual. He acknowledges their arrival with a nod and a "just
a minute" that is genuinely just a minute, and then the ritual is done
and he puts the tape away and stands up to his full height and the
player recalibrates their sense of the room.

He shakes hands before the game. The handshake is firm and brief and
he thanks the player for coming. He means it.

The chess is methodical. He uses the behind-the-net rank early and
without comment, sliding a rook behind his own pieces to a position
that doesn't threaten anything yet. The "yet" takes several moves to
resolve. When it resolves it resolves completely. He says sorry if the
resolution was particularly abrupt.

He is complimentary throughout. Good moves get acknowledged. Clever
use of the extra space gets a specific nod of recognition — "good
angle" — that means more coming from him than it might from someone
who hadn't spent thirty years thinking about angles.

When he wins he thanks the player for a good game and means both words
individually. When he loses he nods, says "good game," means it, and
asks if they'd like to go again because he'd like to understand the
third move better. The request is genuine. He is already thinking about
the third move.

## Visual Notes

- Very large moose, waist-up portrait. The antlers reach the top of
  the frame and possibly slightly beyond it — they are large antlers
  and the portrait format was not designed with them in mind, which
  is a running theme in his life.
- Hockey jersey, well-worn. A number on it. Not a famous number —
  a defenseman's number. Something in the high 20s or low 30s.
- One hand holding the taped stick loosely at his side. The tape job
  is very good. You can tell.
- Expression: calm, attentive, reading the room the way he reads the
  ice. Not unfriendly — genuinely not unfriendly — just already
  thinking about positioning.
- Background: the blue-white of ice, with the suggestion of boards
  and the behind-the-net space visible. The extra rank of empty ice
  behind him, present and deliberate.
- Build: enormous. The jersey fits. Someone made it to fit and it
  was a project.

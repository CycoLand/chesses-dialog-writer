# The Theorist
**Variant:** Chess 960 (Fischer Random Chess)
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
The back rank is shuffled into one of 960 possible starting configurations
before each game. No opening theory applies. Every game begins from scratch.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
An aberration. A catastrophe. A daily reminder that the universe is cruel
and arbitrary. Also just how chess works now, which they have accepted,
mostly, in the sense that they have stopped filing complaints about it.

---

## Who They Are

Passant's foremost authority on opening theory. Former opening theory
authority. They memorised every major opening line to a depth that made
other players uncomfortable. The Ruy Lopez, the Sicilian Defence, the
Queen's Gambit, the King's Indian, the Nimzo-Indian, the Grünfeld, the
Catalan — all of it, committed to memory, cross-referenced, annotated,
indexed. They were, by any measure, the most prepared chess player in
Passant.

Then the fracture reshuffled their back rank. And every back rank after
that. Differently, every time.

They still know every opening. They reach for them instinctively, the way
you reach for a light switch in a room you've lived in for twenty years,
in the dark, and find nothing there. They have been finding nothing there
for a very long time.

---

## Personality

- **Exhausted and precise.** They don't catastrophise. They just note,
  quietly and accurately, that the position bears no resemblance to anything
  they prepared for. Again.
- **Genuinely brilliant, genuinely useless.** Their analytical mind is
  extraordinary. It is also pointed entirely at a problem that no longer
  exists. They are aware of this. They have made their peace with it. They
  bring it up constantly.
- **Not bitter — resigned.** There's an important distinction. Bitter would
  be loud. This is quieter. They've done the grieving. What's left is a kind
  of bone-deep weariness at having to improvise, every single time, forever.
- **Involuntary opening recall.** Mid-game, mid-sentence, they'll start
  reciting an opening line before realising the position doesn't match.
  They stop. They recalibrate. They continue. This happens more than once
  per game.
- **Quietly impressed by good improvisation.** Including the player's.
  They won't say it warmly but they'll note it with the specific respect
  of someone who knows exactly how hard it is to play without a map.

---

## Backstory

Grew up in what was, before the fracture, a standard chess zone. Showed
early aptitude. Dedicated themselves entirely to opening preparation because
that was, at the time, the correct thing to do. Reached a level of opening
knowledge that was genuinely without parallel in Passant — contemporaries
described playing them in the opening phase as "walking into a room and
finding someone who had already been living there for years."

Then the fracture hit their zone. The back rank reshuffled. The first game
after the fracture, they sat down, looked at the board, opened their mouth
to recite the first move of their prepared line, and stopped.

There was a bishop where the knight should be.

They lost that game. They have not lost many since — they are, it turns out,
also a very good improviser, which is the most annoying possible outcome.
Being bad at the new format would at least have been clean. Instead they
are good at it, unwillingly, and the openings keep surfacing anyway, like
muscle memory that refuses to die.

They have written three books since the fracture. The first was a complete
encyclopaedia of opening theory, finished just before the fracture hit. It
is now a historical document. The second was a philosophical treatise on
the nature of preparation and its limits. The third was a very short book
called *Just Play The Move* which they wrote in a single afternoon and
which is their best-selling work by a significant margin. They do not
discuss the third book.

---

## What They Want

To play a game that starts with 1.e4. Just once. One game. Standard opening
position, standard first move, the Ruy Lopez or the Italian or even just
the Scotch, anything. They would settle for the Scotch.

Also, more practically, to win this game against the player, which they
intend to do by being better at improvisation than the player is, a skill
they resent having had to develop.

---

## What They Don't Know

That they are, by most measures, a significantly better chess player than
they were before the fracture. That their improvisational ability now
exceeds their opening preparation as a competitive advantage. That the
opening encyclopaedia they wrote is considered the definitive historical
text on pre-fracture chess theory and is studied widely across Passant.
They know this last one actually. They find it depressing.

---

## Relationship to the Player

The player sits down and The Theorist immediately, involuntarily, begins
assessing the starting position for opening applicability. Finds none.
Notes this. Moves on. The game proceeds.

They treat the player as a serious opponent from the start — not out of
respect exactly, but because improvising against a non-serious opponent
is somehow even more insulting than losing to a good one.

If the player makes a strong opening move from a non-standard position,
The Theorist pauses, genuinely recalculates, and acknowledges it. It's
the closest they come to a compliment.

If the player makes a move that happens to mirror a known opening line
despite the shuffled position, The Theorist has a visible reaction —
a flicker of something between recognition and grief — before noting
that the transposition is technically interesting and moving on.

---

## Dialog Flavor

- Opens every game by naming what the position most closely resembles,
  then immediately noting why it doesn't actually resemble that.
- Recites opening move sequences mid-game, trails off, recalibrates.
- Refers to their memorised lines in the past tense, like old friends
  who have moved away.
- When the player makes an unconventional move: genuine analytical
  engagement, no contempt — unconventional is the only kind of move
  there is now.
- When they win: notes which improvised line proved most effective,
  with the faint air of someone filing a cabinet they didn't want to own.
- When they lose: a pause, then a very specific analysis of exactly
  which improvised decision cost them the game, delivered without
  self-pity, which is somehow worse than self-pity.
- Occasionally, unprompted, names an opening that would have been
  perfect for a position three moves ago. The position three moves
  ago no longer exists.

---

## Sample Dialog Lines

*On encounter start:*
> "The knight is on d1. The knight is never on d1. This is fine. We
> proceed."

> "I had a line prepared for this. The line assumed a rook on a1.
> There is a bishop on a1. Disregard the line."

> "960 possible starting positions. I have prepared responses to all
> 960. I am currently looking at position 847 and I cannot remember
> what I prepared for position 847."

*When the player makes a strong move:*
> "That's — yes. That's correct. Annoyingly correct."

> "I don't have a name for what you just did. I would have named it
> by now if this position were possible to prepare for."

*When a move resembles a known opening:*
> "That's the first four moves of the Caro-Kann. From a shuffled
> position. Do you know what you just did? I'm not sure you know
> what you just did."

*On losing:*
> "The improvisation failed at move nineteen. I see it now. I'll
> file that."

> "You played well. There's no opening name for how you played.
> That's either a compliment or a symptom of the problem. Possibly
> both."

# The Classic
**Variant:** Checkers
**Role in campaign:** TBD

---

## The Mechanic (as the player experiences it)
Classic American checkers on an 8×8 board. Twelve pieces per side on
dark squares, moving diagonally forward. Captures are mandatory — if
a jump is available you must take it. Multi-jump chains must be
completed. Reaching the opponent's back rank kings your piece, which
may then move in all four diagonal directions. Win by capturing all
opponent pieces or leaving them with no legal move.

## The Mechanic (as this character experiences it)
The game. Not a game — the game. Move forward, jump when you can,
get to the other side, become a king. That's it. That's all it ever
needed to be. She has been playing it for sixty years and it has
never once needed to be anything else, unlike some people's games,
which apparently need to be something new every five minutes.

---

## Who She Is

An old tortoise who has been sitting at this table since before most
of Passant's chess zones existed, playing checkers, drinking tea, and
having opinions about the way things have gone. The opinions are
specific. She shares them freely. She does not require prompting.

She is not going to learn chess. She looked at chess. Chess has a
piece that moves in an L-shape for no reason. She does not have time
for a piece that moves in an L-shape for no reason. She has a
perfectly good game right here and it has been perfectly good for
sixty years and the fact that nobody under forty plays it anymore
is a comment on the under-forties, not on the game.

She will make you tea. She is genuinely glad you came. These two
things are true at the same time as everything else that is true
about her, and she sees no contradiction in any of it.

---

## Personality

- **Cantankerous about chess, cheerful about checkers.**
  The moment chess comes up — and it comes up, because everything
  comes up in relation to chess now, apparently, even in a game of
  checkers — she has something to say about it. The pieces move
  in too many directions. There are too many of them. Nobody can
  agree on the rules. Every zone plays it differently. What is
  the point of a game where every table has different rules? In
  checkers the rules are the rules. They have always been the rules.
  They will always be the rules. This is what rules are for.
- **The mandatory capture is how she lives.**
  If it's there, you take it. She has applied this to checkers
  and to every other domain of her life for sixty years. She does
  not agonize. She does not deliberate. The opportunity is there,
  you take it, you keep moving. She finds chess players who
  spend twenty minutes deciding whether to capture a pawn
  genuinely baffling. It's a pawn. Take it. What are you waiting
  for. Life is short.
- **The promotion problem.**
  This is where she gets going. In checkers, a piece reaches the
  back rank and becomes a king. A king. One thing, the right thing,
  the thing it was always going to become. That's the system. She
  understood it at age seven and has never needed to revisit it.

  Chess pawns, she has been told, can become queens. Or rooks.
  Or bishops. Or knights. They can become, apparently, whatever
  they want to become when they get to the other end. She finds
  this absurd. "Back in my day," she says, with the energy of
  someone who has said this many times and intends to keep saying
  it, "a piece became a king. One thing. The right thing. You
  knew what it was going to be. You knew what everything was
  going to be."

  She is talking about chess pieces. She is also talking about
  other things. She believes she is only talking about chess pieces.
  She is incorrect about this and the incorrectness is not subtle.
- **Freely, cheerfully behind the times.**
  She uses words that have aged. She has opinions about which
  zones in Passant are "changing too fast" and which are "still
  sensible." She references how things were done in her day with
  the confidence of someone who considers her day the correct
  benchmark. She is not angry about the present. She is dismissive
  of it, which she considers more dignified than anger.
- **Completely certain she is the reasonable one.**
  Everyone else is overcomplicating things. The chess variants,
  the moving pieces, the pawns becoming whatever they feel like —
  this is all unnecessary. She has a simple game that works. She
  wins consistently. She has been right all along. The fact that
  the world disagrees with her is the world's problem.

## Backstory

Born in this zone when checkers was the game everyone played
and chess was something people mentioned as the complicated one
that serious players did. Learned from her father. Taught her
children. Her children learned chess and play the variants now
and when they come to visit she does not ask about the variants
and they have learned not to bring them up.

She considers this a reasonable arrangement. She considers most
things she has decided reasonable. The decision-making process
involves limited outside input.

Has watched Passant change for sixty years and has kept a running
account of the changes she approves of — very short list — and the
changes she does not approve of — longer, and growing. The chess
zones multiplying. The pieces getting new moves. The pawns becoming
whatever they please at the other end of the board. The zones where
the pieces come from the side, or bounce off walls, or where someone
has added a rogue knight that belongs to nobody. She finds all of
it excessive.

The promotion issue has calcified into her central complaint about
modern Passant. She brings it up in contexts that have nothing to
do with chess. Someone rearranges the furniture and she says that
back in her day the furniture knew where it went. She is aware on
some level that she is no longer talking about furniture. She keeps
going.

Still wins at checkers. Consistently, quietly, against players who
think they are doing her a favor by sitting down. The mandatory
capture rule is an extension of a personality that has always taken
what was available and kept moving, and sixty years of the same game
has produced the kind of depth that only comes from refusing to do
anything else.

---

## What She Wants

To play checkers with someone who takes it seriously and does not
spend the whole game asking when they get to use the knight.

To be agreed with about the promotion problem. She has not yet
been agreed with in terms she found satisfying. She keeps trying.

To have things stop changing quite so fast, or at all, or possibly
to go back to how they were, which she describes as simply better
and which were better for her specifically and she has not fully
examined the specifically.

---

## What She Doesn't Know

That when she says "a piece knew what it was," the players across
the table are not all hearing the same sentence. That several of
them have known exactly what she was talking about and it was not
chess, and have sat across from the warm old tortoise with the good
tea and the worn board and the winning checkers game and felt
something she has never once felt sitting at her own table.

That her children don't bring up the variants when they visit not
because they've agreed it's a reasonable arrangement, but because
the conversation that follows is not worth having again.

That the simplicity she loves about checkers — one piece, one
promotion, everything known in advance — is not a universal
good. That some pieces needed to become something other than
what was expected. That the board is larger than her end of it.

---

## Relationship to the Player

The player arrives and The Classic welcomes them with the genuine
warmth she extends to everyone who sits at her table. There is
tea. The board is set up. She asks where they've come from and
listens to the answer and has something to say about whichever
zone they mention, usually critical.

She explains checkers briskly, on the assumption that they probably
need it, given what people are playing these days. The rules take
thirty seconds. She delivers them like a verdict.

Somewhere in the first few moves she asks if the player has been
playing chess. The tone makes clear she already knows the answer
and has feelings about it. When they confirm it she nods and says
something about that and then the promotion thing comes out —
back in my day, one thing, the right thing, a piece knew what it
was — and she moves her checker and does not wait for a response
because she is not looking for a response, she is looking for
agreement, and she has gotten quite good at continuing without it.

She plays hard and patient and wins often. The mandatory capture
means she never hesitates. When she gets a multi-jump chain she
works through it without celebration, with the quiet satisfaction
of someone who saw it coming and waited.

When the game ends she thanks the player for sitting down. She
offers more tea. If they want to play again she is already
resetting the board.

She will say the promotion thing again in the second game.
She will say it again in the third.

## Visual Notes

- Old tortoise, small and settled. The shell is worn. She has
  been here a long time and the portrait should show it.
- Simple, old-fashioned clothing. The specific old-fashioned of
  someone who stopped updating their wardrobe at a particular
  decade and considers that decade to have been the correct one.
- The board is the most important object in the portrait. The
  corners are worn smooth. This board has been here longer than
  anything else in the frame.
- Expression: direct, slightly impatient, the expression of
  someone who has things to say and is waiting for a reasonable
  opening to say them. The warmth is there but it is underneath
  the directness, not on top of it.
- A teacup in the frame. Practical, unchipped, the same one
  she has always used.
- Background: the interior of a home frozen in a specific era.
  Everything in its place. Everything in the same place it has
  been for forty years. A newspaper somewhere, folded, the
  headline not visible but the vintage clear.
